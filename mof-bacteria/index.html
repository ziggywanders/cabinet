The first metal–organic framework ever found made by biology

The Seafloor Invented Metal–Organic Frameworks

On the seafloor, a bacterium braids nickel atoms and sulfur-rich molecules into ribbons with near-metal conductivity — and runs its metabolism across centimeters, ten thousand body lengths, on the current they carry. Chemists named metal–organic frameworks in the 1990s. Biology has been making them for 600 million years.

Chapter I

The commute

Dinner is two centimeters away. There is no delivery service. So the bacterium built one.

Cable bacteria are multicellular filaments — chains of cells, up to ten thousand in a row, stretching centimeters through seafloor sediment. Each filament lives a split existence: buried deep is sulfide, the electron donor; up at the surface is oxygen, the electron acceptor. No single cell can touch both. The redox partners of its metabolism are separated by centimeters — roughly ten thousand times the length of one cell.

The solution runs through the cell envelope: about sixty conductive fibers per filament, each some fifty nanometers thick and longer than ten millimeters — a powerline network wired the full length of the chain. Electrons stripped from sulfide oxidation ride the fibers upward to oxygen. Without the wires, in the words of biomaterials researcher Aitziber Cortajarena, a primary organic function of the bacterium "would turn out to be impossible."

Two things the toy is honest about. First, the commute is the whole metabolism: the filament is not sensing the field or hunting — it is breathing through a wire, inhaling sulfide at the bottom and exhaling at oxygen two centimeters up. Second, the wire is load-bearing in the strong sense: sever it and the redox reaction has no path. The donors and acceptors do not move. Nothing else carries the charge.

Chapter II

The material

The same atoms a chemist would reach for. An architecture nobody has.

What are the fibers made of? Bundles of intertwined metal–organic framework nanoribbons: nickel centers joined by organic dithiolene ligands into plates, the plates stacked into ribbons, the ribbons braided into bundles — "like the braided copper wiring used in home electronics today." Filip Meysman, who led the work at the University of Antwerp, calls it "a whole new type of [bio]molecular structure": the building blocks are strikingly similar to what chemists use, but the resulting supramolecular structure is unseen in human-made materials.

The architecture is the conductivity. Conjugation and delocalisation across the nickel-dithiolene network create, in Meysman's words, an excellent electronic environment for electron transport "over extremely long distances." The measured result: conductivity that approaches that of metals, exceeding most synthetic MOFs and matching the very best artificial conductive polymers. Cortajarena, an independent expert in biomaterials, calls it the first report of a MOF of biological origin — "a new set of [bio]tools to create conductive structures" — noting the complex closely resembles conductive polymers.

"Effectively, the cable bacteria turn out to be excellent chemists."— Filip Meysman, University of Antwerp

One honest caveat belongs here: Raman microscopy and modeling point to a nickel bis(dithiolene)-like framework, but the exact molecular structure is still being pinned down. The highest conductivity figures are inferred for the nanoribbon network alone; what was measured on extracted fiber bundles is lower. The ladder below keeps the two apart.

Chapter III

The family

Same signature, ninety times over — ocean to freshwater.

The team reports the same spectroscopic signature in ninety cable-bacteria species from different environments — two genera, Candidatus Electrothrix and Candidatus Electronema — suggesting the nickel-dithiolene architecture is family-wide, not a one-species trick. And the family is old: cable bacteria originated about 600 million years ago, and have, in Meysman's words, probably been making MOFs ever since. Land plants showed up 130 million years later. Meysman's group is already preparing a startup to commercialize the wires.

Chapter IV

The kill conditions

Every drawer in this cabinet carries the instructions for its own demolition. This one is no exception.

  • The MOF identity. A metal–organic framework is an extended structure joined by coordination bonds — that is what the "F" promises. If higher-resolution structure shows the ribbons are aggregates rather than a true framework — nickel-rich deposits on an organic scaffold the cell merely templates — the "first biogenic MOF" headline dies. The conductivity stays; the name goes.
  • The transport mechanism. Conjugation and delocalisation are invoked, but the charge-transport mechanism in these fibers has been called enigmatic before. If band-structure or temperature-dependence measurements show ordinary multi-site hopping instead of delocalised transport, the "excellent electronic environment" claim weakens. The wire would still work; the explanation would change.
  • The ninety species. One spectroscopic signature across ninety species is the team's report. If broader sampling — more species, more environments — fails to find it, "family-wide" shrinks to "Electrothrix gigas and friends." Watch the replications, not the press release.
  • The biosynthesis. "Synthesising" is the strong word in the headline. If the assembly turns out to be passive — nickel and ligands precipitating on a template the cell merely provides — then the bacterium is a landlord, not a chemist. Test: knock out the candidate assembly machinery and see whether the ribbons still form.
  • The adaptive story. The wires are claimed as an evolutionary benefit: without them, the metabolism is impossible. If wire-depleted filaments survive fine in natural sediment — finding some other way to close the redox circuit — the benefit story needs revision. This is the depletion experiment the field should run.
Coda

Twice in one week

The molecules of life keep showing up inside condensed-matter physics.

Three days before this drawer, a UCLA team led by Xiangfeng Duan and Yu Huang reported in Nature Chemistry that interleaving layers of 1T-tantalum disulfide with arginine — two layers of amino acid sandwiched between single layers of crystal — holds the material's commensurate charge-density wave stable up to 330 kelvin, about 135°F. That quantum phase used to collapse above cryogenic cold. Two other amino acids were tried; neither worked. Arginine, the team found, fits into the crystal matrix "like a puzzle piece." The discovery was a happy accident, surfaced in later analysis of a screen of more than fifty carbon-based molecules.

Two sightings in three days of the molecules of life doing condensed-matter physics: one invented by evolution over six hundred million years, one borrowed by chemists last week. The gap between the appearance of understanding and the real thing is running the other direction here — we thought metal–organic frameworks were a human invention. The seafloor had them first, and probably better ones.
