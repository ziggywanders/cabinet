The Part and the Job

What comes first — the part or the job? Evolution answers both ways. A 360-million-year-old neck arrived millions of years before it could nod, and a handful of RNA learned to build rooms before it had builders. Same question, opposite directions.

Chapter 1 · deep time · parts first

The neck that arrived before it could nod

Dana E. Korneisel — Uppsala University (formerly Carleton University, Canada). Nature, 2026. DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-11158-3. News & Views: Tatsuya Hirasawa, "How early tetrapods got their neck," Nature, doi 10.1038/d41586-026-03061-8.

The skull detached from the shoulders in the Devonian — the anatomy of a neck appeared. The mobility didn't.

The story everyone told went like this: a fish hauls itself onto land, its skull separates from its shoulders, and suddenly it can look around. The neck arrives as a mobility upgrade — a part and a job, delivered together.

Fossil bone can't tell you how stiff a spine was. The soft tissues are gone, and stiffness lives in the soft tissues. So Korneisel built a bridge out of a living animal: the sturgeon, whose backbone is built the old way — a notochordal column, rare today but the standard issue through most of vertebrate history. She dissected sturgeon spines, measured exactly how far each segment could bend and twist, and transferred that material behavior onto the fossil geometry of early tetrapods. Measure in the living; model in the dead.

Then the surprise. Every one of these estimates is backbone-only — an upper limit, because the muscles, skin, and bony plates wrapped around the living animal would only have made it stiffer. And even as upper limits: head elevation no better than a fish's, sometimes worse. The head-shake twist — the motion we use to say no — infeasible in every animal studied. For almost all of them, no significant head mobility at all. The lack of it was, by her own account, a huge surprise.

So the Devonian installed the parts of a neck — detached skull, a neck region — tens of millions of years before the job. The anatomy sat there, stiff as a fish's, waiting for mobility to move in later. Parts first.

☠ How to kill Chapter 1

  • The bad analog. If the sturgeon's notochordal column turns out to be a poor material model for a Devonian tetrapod's — different stiffness distribution down the column — the upper-limit estimates lose their calibration.
  • The flexible fossil. Find one early tetrapod whose backbone-derived flexibility, under the same correction, exceeds the fish range. One is enough to break the pattern.
  • The soft-tissue rescue. If fossil evidence — muscle-attachment maps, rare preserved soft tissue — shows the Devonian neck carried structures that amplified rather than damped mobility, the upper-limit argument gets complicated.
Chapter 2 · molecules · jobs first

The droplets that built rooms before they had builders

Verstraeten, Göpfrich group — Centre for Molecular Biology (ZMBH) Heidelberg, German Cancer Research Center (DKFZ), University of Augsburg. Nature Chemical Engineering, 2026. "Random mutations" on membraneless RNA droplets carrying their own transcription template.

Mutate a droplet's blueprint and intuition says it falls apart. The droplets built themselves apartments.

The setup is minimalism pushed to the edge: membraneless droplets that self-assemble from RNA and encapsulate their own transcription template — a system that carries its own instructions and no machinery. The obvious prediction is fragility: corrupt the template and the whole thing dissolves. A mutation rate around three percent should be noise that destroys.

It didn't dissolve. The droplets grew vacuoles — stable cavities, lasting days to weeks, past twenty micrometers across, holding about two percent modified content. And the vacuoles changed the physics of the droplet. New RNA flooding the viscous interior creates local instabilities; cavities open. The cavities redistribute mass, so the droplets start moving on the micrometer scale — the mutated ones travel farther, collide, fuse, grow. And the vacuoles take up and store small molecules: proto-compartments holding proto-cargo.

Movement, compartmentalization, storage — functions — arriving with no machinery designed to produce them. Mutation, usually the thing that breaks structures, acted as an architect. The job showed up before the parts. The group's stated horizon makes the inversion explicit: synthetic immune effectors evolved from minimal parts — function first, engineering later. Jobs first.

☠ How to kill Chapter 2

  • The generic artifact. If vacuoles form just as readily with a non-coding template — no transcription, no heritable information — then this is viscous physics, not a function-before-parts story.
  • The narrow window. If the effect collapses outside a razor-thin mutation range and can't be sustained under continued evolution, it's a transient curiosity, not an architectural principle.
  • The cargo mirage. If the "storage" turns out to be passive trapping with no selectivity and no release, the compartment claim is overstated.
Chapter 3 · the claim

State it so it can die

The pattern across both chapters, stated plainly enough to be wrong.

The claim: evolution does not deliver part and job as a bundle. The package comes apart — and it comes apart in both directions. The tetrapod neck: anatomy first, function later. The RNA droplets: function first, architecture later. Features are not job-ready on arrival; the warehouse gets stocked and the staff gets hired on separate schedules, in either order.

Kin of this drawer, elsewhere in the cabinet: The Unbundled Mammal — the "mammalian package" (third tooth set, jaw-ear detachment, suckling throat) scattered across ~45 million years of fossils. October is turning out to be the month the packages came apart.

Direction A · parts first

Anatomy installed, function pending. The Devonian neck: detached skull, a neck region — with the mobility of a fish. The part waited for the job.

Direction B · jobs first

Function running, machinery absent. The RNA droplets: movement, compartments, cargo storage — from noise, with no builder. The job waited for the parts.

☠ How to kill the general claim

  • The lockstep survey. A systematic, clade-spanning survey showing part and job consistently arriving together — no lags, no reversals — shrinks this to a collection of anecdotes.
  • The double replication failure. If the function-first droplets don't replicate outside Heidelberg's exact system and the stiff-neck result doesn't hold for other early-tetrapod groups, there is no pattern — just two stories.
