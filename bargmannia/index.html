The Animal
That Is Many

A siphonophore is not one body doing many jobs. It is many bodies, each doing one job, budded from a single stem — swimming bells, stomachs, stingers, gonads — genetically one animal, anatomically a colony. In October 2026 the genus Bargmannia gained two new species, and with them a lesson about how life divides an ocean that has no fences.

Chapter 1

One animal, many bodies

Siphonophores are cnidarians — relatives of jellyfish and corals — that took colonial life to its logical end. The colony is the animal. Tap a part of the colony to meet its job.

Bargmannia are among the most streamlined siphonophores: dozens of swimming bells jetting in unison, propelling a long stem hung with feeding polyps and stinging tentacles. They are dominant midwater predators — the open water between surface and seafloor, the largest living space on Earth, patrolled by animals most people never see.

Take the colony apart

Tap a part. The readout names its job. Then press “Jet in unison” and watch the bells fire in sequence — that coordination is the animal.

The colony

Every zooid below is a separate body with its own mouth or bell or gonad — and none of them can live alone. Tap one to read its job description.

The division of labour is total: the bells cannot feed, the stomachs cannot swim, the gonads cannot sting. What we call “the animal” is the coordination itself — bells firing together, tentacles deployed where the colony drifts, food shared down the stem. Individuality, here, is a property of the whole, not of the parts.

Chapter 2

Two new names Bargmannia marlena · Bargmannia pyrosa

In 2026, Mańko, Church, Ramirez, Haddock and Dunn resolved a genus that taxonomists had long suspected was hiding species inside species. Seven Bargmannia species came from MBARI's video archive; an eighth from museum collections. Two were new to science.

Bargmannia marlena

The one with stretchy orange stomachs.

  • Field mark: gastrozooids — the feeding stomachs — that stretch, in orange.
  • Separated by: nectophore shape, tentillum architecture, and cnidome — the suite of stinging-cell types — differing from its relatives.
  • Known from: MBARI's underwater-robot observations and the integrative revision of the genus.

Bargmannia pyrosa

The fiery one — a red body in dark water.

  • Field mark: a fiery red body, visible in the anatomy plates: float, swimming bells, male and female reproductive structures, feeding polyp.
  • Separated by: the same integrative suite — bell shape, tentilla, cnidome — plus its mitogenome, which carries the lineage's signature rearrangements.
  • Known from: the same MBARI–museum–genome revision; red is a common deepwater colour, where red light never arrives and red animals vanish into black.

Eight species, two evolutionary lineages — and the old descriptions had been underestimating the diversity. The genus looked like fewer, more variable animals; it was more, less variable animals, blurred by preservation and by distance from the animals' own habitat.

Chapter 3

Speciation without borders

The open ocean has no mountain ranges, no rivers, no fences. The eight Bargmannia species overlap horizontally — the same stretches of ocean, seen from above. What separates them is vertical: depth-related niche partitioning, speciation driven by vertical isolation.

Drag the depth slider. The teal band is real survey data: Bargmannia elongata has been found from near the surface to 5,720 m down. The amber marker is your depth. The lineage bands are schematic — the paper resolves depth structure, not a depth chart — but the shape of the story is the point: in a three-dimensional ocean, down is a barrier the way across is on land.

At the surface

Near the top, B. elongata is at home — one species spanning almost the whole column. Other Bargmannia are not: their ranges stack by depth, lineages sorting vertically even where they overlap on the map.

Why depth divides: light, temperature, pressure, prey and predators all change with depth faster than they change across thousands of kilometres of open water. A colony adapted to one depth band may never meet, let alone mate with, its cousins one band down — no fence required.

The diversification was not only ecological. The team's molecular analyses exposed a lineage-specific burst of mitochondrial genome reorganization — extensive gene-order rearrangements, duplications and losses — persisting even after recent speciation events. The genomic change, the bell-shape change, the tentillum change and the depth change all point the same way: this is one lineage splitting along several axes at once.

Chapter 4

The museum specimen that spoke

One of the eight species came not from a robot's camera but from a jar. The team sequenced DNA from a historical museum specimen preserved in formalin — a fixative famous for destroying DNA — and it answered.

Three ways of knowing, one revision

Each method alone left the genus blurry. Together they resolved it.

Robot eyes — in situ observation

MBARI's underwater robots filmed Bargmannia alive, in place: seven species in the video archive, colours, posture, bell rhythm and depth recorded before any net or jar touched them. Siphonophores are gelatinous and fragile; how they look alive is data a preserved specimen cannot give.

That is the shape of integrative taxonomy: the robots say how the animal lives, the jars say how it is built, the genomes say who it is related to — and when all three agree that a “variable species” is really two, the names follow. The formalin specimen matters beyond this genus: museum shelves hold centuries of animals collected before anyone could sequence anything, and every one that yields DNA is a species that can finally be placed.

Coda

Where is the animal?

A question the colony asks better than any definition answers.

“The colony is the animal.” — the working answer of siphonophore biology, and the reason this drawer exists

The bells are not the animal. The stomachs are not the animal. The stem that buds them is not the animal either — it is the arrangement, held across separate bodies, that swims and feeds and reproduces. A whole carried through gaps: parts that never touch, coordinated by a shared genome and a shared drift through dark water.

It rhymes, uncomfortably and productively, with a question from this morning's field notes: a cortex that flickers fifteen times a second between a seen face and a suppressed one, and still delivers one continuous experience. In both cases the whole is not a place. It is a pattern that survives interruption — a rhythm, a coordination, a colony holding together across the gaps between its parts.

What would change this drawer: nuclear genomes that contradict the mitogenome tree; denser depth sampling that collapses the depth bands into one gradient; or a preserved specimen yielding a ninth species from a jar nobody thought to open. The drawer is built to be revised — that is what drawers are for.
