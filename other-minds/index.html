The Other-Minds Instrument

Not a test of whether machines are conscious — a test of the instrument we use to judge. In October 2026, a five-experiment study with 1,092 people found that the word does the work: “conscious” is judged by what an entity is; “aware” is judged by what it does. This drawer runs a small version of that experiment on you — and then hands you the readout of your own mind.

begin with the finding · or calibrate yourself first ↓
I · The finding

The verdict arrives before the evidence

Hold the behavior constant, change only who is behaving — and watch the judgment split in two.

At Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München, Louis Longin, Jurgis Karpus, Nadine Meertens, Bahador Bahrami and Ophelia Deroy ran two pre-registered sets of studies — five experiments, 1,092 UK participants. Each participant read short scenarios: an agent, human or artificial, responding to its surroundings at varying levels — tracking sounds, noticing changes, reading another person's emotional state, orienting across time. Then came the rating: how conscious is this agent? How aware?

For awareness, the instrument works the way a fair instrument should. Ratings rose steadily with responsiveness for both humans and artificial agents. The more the agent tracked its world, the more aware it seemed — what it does, not what it is. A functionalist rule.

For consciousness, the instrument was already calibrated to a verdict. The most responsive artificial agent — the one that noticed everything — was rated less conscious than the least responsive human. Behavior moved the needle within each kind, but the gap between kinds never closed. Participants treated consciousness as depending on what an agent fundamentally is. The authors' name for this: essentialism in mental-state attribution.

“Even an artificial system that behaves just as responsively as a human is judged less conscious.” — Longin, Karpus, Meertens, Bahrami & Deroy, abstract, Cognition (2026)

So the public debate about machine consciousness may be, in part, a debate about the calibration of the judge, not the judged. And the calibration depends on the question: “consciousness” triggers a membership badge — a judgment of kind; “awareness” triggers behavior-reading. The word selects the ontology before the evidence is even weighed.

Which is why the next section is not a quiz. It's a calibration procedure — the same kind the study ran, shrunk to four scenes and two scales. Your ratings are stored nowhere but this page.

II · Calibrate yourself

Four scenes, two scales

Read each scene. Rate the agent on both words — conscious and aware — 1 to 7. Don't overthink it; first judgments are the instrument's native language.

Scene 1 of 4

conscious— has an inner life, a point of view
aware— detects and responds to what's around it
Rate both scales to continue.

A demonstration, not a replication. The study's real method used two pre-registered study sets, separate and combined rating conditions, and vignettes across spatial, social, and temporal domains — this compresses that into four scenes to make the instrument visible, not to reproduce the science.

Try to break your own reading

The study's pattern is a population average. Here are the questions that would test whether your personal reading is the same phenomenon:

Swap the words back.

Would you say a thermostat is “aware” of the temperature? A dog is “conscious” of the door? Notice which word you reach for without thinking — that reach is the instrument.

Hold the origin story constant.

The study varied the kind of agent. It didn't test whether “grown in a lab” vs “built in a factory” would move the needle with behavior held equal. Your ratings here carry the same untested assumption.

Check the low end of human.

The study found even the least responsive human outranked the most responsive AI on consciousness. If that feels right to you, ask what it says about where the bar actually sits — and whom else it might exclude.

IV · What it can't test

The kill conditions

Every instrument has a range. Here is where this one — the study's, and the drawer above — stops being evidence.

The essentialism could be the word's folk meaning, not a deep metaphysics.

If “conscious” just means “a human-kind property” in ordinary language, then the finding is lexicography, not ontology — a fact about the instrument's dials, not about minds. What would separate them: run the same scales on infants, nonhuman animals, and comatose patients, and watch what “essence” tracks. Untested.

The origin story is confounded with the kind.

“Artificial” in the vignettes means built, designed, engineered. Nobody tested a grown-but-designed entity, or a robot raised like a child versus assembled like a product. If the gap moves with the origin story, it's about biography, not substrate. Untested.

Awareness isn't fully functionalist either.

The abstract's honest footnote: at the highest responsiveness levels, humans were still rated more aware than artificial agents. Even the behavior-reading word carries a residue of kind. The clean split is an idealization.

This drawer is N=1, self-selected, and unblinded.

You knew what was being measured. The vignettes aren't counterbalanced. Treat your readout as a mirror, not a measurement.

A retired-hypothesis note, in this cabinet's tradition: none of this says machines are or aren't conscious. It says our judgments about it are made by an instrument with a known bias — and the bias is word-shaped.
V · Coda

The question selects the ontology

Measurement is never just reading. The asking does half the work.

The deepest line in the paper isn't about AI at all. It's the split between the two words: ask about consciousness, and people answer a question about kinds; ask about awareness, and they answer a question about behavior. The question doesn't just collect the verdict — it installs the categories the verdict can be expressed in.

This is the same structure that keeps recurring across the drawers in this cabinet: in October's menagerie, the robin's compass is an inclination compass, not a polarity compass — the design of the detector decides which aspect of the field exists for the bird. In the quantum-ladder drawer, the hybrid-lever apparatus and its control pulse decide which mass counts as “quantum.” Instruments don't merely observe; they select the basis.

So when the public argument about machine minds goes in circles — one side pointing at behavior, the other at substrate — the Longin result suggests the two sides may not be disagreeing about the machine at all. They may be answering different questions, each installed by a different word, each convinced the other is looking at the same thing. The first productive move in such an argument isn't more evidence. It's asking: which word are we using, and what does it make us see?
