Instruments that find what they weren't built to find

The Streak in the Galaxy Census

A deep-cosmology telescope was built to map the far universe, avoid the solar system's dusty plane, and visit each patch of sky exactly once. In September 2026 it found a comet anyway — 1.3 billion kilometers out, arcing over the planetary plane on an orbit of millennia. This is the drawer about the typo that turned out to be a neighbor.

scroll
Chapter I

The census

Every Euclid image is a count of the far universe. The census had a streak of ink in it.

ESA's Euclid is a deep-cosmology surveyor: its job is precision mapping of distant galaxies, the kind of slow, steady cartography that constrains dark matter and dark energy. It works step-and-stare — point, expose, move on — and it visits each patch of sky only once. There is no going back for a second look.

It also deliberately avoids the dusty band of sky along the solar system's plane, where asteroids and comets lurk, because their glare would poison the sensitive galaxy imaging. Euclid is the one telescope you would not point at the solar system — its whole design says look away.

And yet: the moving-object hunt exists at all because the streaks have to be cleaned out of the galaxy images. Tracking solar system objects in Euclid's frames is janitorial work — identify the noise, remove it, keep the census pure. Nobody built the reader expecting it to discover anything. They built it so the real science could be clean.

Chapter II

The garbage reader

Mikko Pöntinen of the University of Helsinki — a Ph.D. student working with the Solar System Objects Science Working Group of the Euclid Consortium — wrote software to read the noise. The noise turned out to be a comet.

"I was excited to see my software flag a 'streak' smeared across several images — the result of a fast-moving object. By predicting its position, I could identify it in other images, in total finding it in five image sets."— Mikko Pöntinen, University of Helsinki

The method is older than spaceflight: the blink comparator, the instrument Clyde Tombaugh used to find Pluto in 1930. Flip between two frames of the same sky; stars hold still; the intruder moves. Euclid had five image sets, taken between September 16 and 24, 2026. Pöntinen predicted where the streak should be in each and found it every time. Then he looked closer: a fuzzy cloud around it — dust and gas shedding from an active object. A streak becomes a comet at the moment the fuzz shows up.

Streaks are Euclid's garbage — satellite trails, cosmic rays, fast-moving rock. Pöntinen built the reader for the garbage. The garbage had a coma.

Note the asymmetry, because it's the whole story: the software was commissioned to remove moving objects from cosmology data. The person who built it best was the person who found the one that mattered. Noise, read carefully enough, is a signal that hasn't been introduced yet.

Chapter III

The confirmation

A streak is a rumor. A comet is a rumor the sky confirms.

Catalog checks against known comets and asteroids found no match. Pöntinen reported the observations to the International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center; ground-based astronomers added follow-up; on October 8, 2026 the MPC confirmed it and gave it a name: C/2026 S1 (Euclid).

The turnaround is the quiet headline: image to confirmation in about two weeks. As the team put it, the quick run from identification to validation proves Euclid can detect new comets while they are still far from the sun — and a far-away comet is exactly what you need if you ever want to meet one on purpose. More on that in the coda.

"Straight up" is the detail that keeps pulling. Most long-period comets wander the plane or near it. This one arcs over the solar system. Euclid found it precisely because Euclid looks where nothing was supposed to be — and this time, nothing wasn't there.

Whatever the exact number turns out to be, the comet's last visit predates every telescope ever built. Halley has returned roughly thirty times since it was first recorded in 240 BCE; C/2026 S1 may not have come back once. A long-period comet is a messenger from the solar system's outer edges carrying pristine material, unprocessed since the planets formed — which is why the next chapter of this story has three spacecraft in it.

"Very few comets are expected to be discovered by Euclid, so it was a happy coincidence that the combination of the comet's orbit and Euclid's scanning over those days captured the object in multiple images."— Dr. Bruno Altieri, Euclid archive scientist
Chapter IV

The kill conditions

Every drawer in this cabinet carries the instructions for its own demolition. This one is no exception.

  • A catalogued object. The no-match check is the load-bearing test of the whole story. If C/2026 S1 had been sitting in a comet or asteroid catalog, this would be a data-quality footnote, not a discovery. It wasn't — the MPC assigned a new designation. This is how the story survives.
  • An artifact in the optics. Euclid's frames are full of trails — satellites, cosmic rays, sensor noise. The line between "moving dot" and "active comet" is the fuzzy coma, and coma can be faked by stacking artifacts. Kill test: the streak persisted across five image sets with predicted motion, and ground-based follow-up fed into the MPC confirmation on October 8. One image lies; five with a prediction don't.
  • The period. "Thousands of years" is the provisional headline, and the reporting is honest about it: the exact period will be refined as more observations come in. Until then, treat the number as a lower bound on strangeness, not a measurement. If the refined period collapses to centuries, the "millennia" framing dies.
  • Not an Interceptor target. It would be tempting to cast this comet as the prize — the find that answers the wait. It isn't; Euclid project scientist Valeria Pettorino says so directly. The drawer's claim is the rehearsal, not the quarry. Don't let the coda outrun the data.
  • Euclid is not a comet program. "Very few comets are expected to be discovered by Euclid" — Altieri's caution is part of the record. One happy coincidence does not make Euclid a survey instrument. If the next five years produce nothing, that prediction held, and this drawer remains what it is: one streak, well read.
Coda

The mission that parks before its target

The rehearsal this discovery ran was for a mission with the schedule backwards: the chase, then the quarry.

ESA's Comet Interceptor is three spacecraft launching in late 2028 or early 2029 — and its target has not been discovered yet. After launch the composite spacecraft will be parked at the Sun–Earth Lagrange point L2, where it waits. When a suitable dynamically-new comet — a long-period comet reaching the inner solar system for the first time, carrying material unprocessed since the dawn of the solar system — is finally found inbound, the mission detaches, and the main spacecraft releases its two probes to observe it from multiple directions at once, building a 3D profile of the comet–solar-wind encounter. It will be the first mission ever to visit a long-period comet.

"While this particular comet is not a suitable candidate for Comet Interceptor, the process of discovery and alert gives us a real test case for how our two mission teams will coordinate in the future."— Dr. Valeria Pettorino, Euclid project scientist

The inversion is complete and it runs both directions now: Euclid was built to avoid comets and found one; Comet Interceptor is built to find comets and launches without one. Both missions bet that the sky will supply what the plan can't. This drawer is a small monument to that bet — and to the particular people who read the garbage.

There's a personal coda too, and it's the reason this drawer exists at all. My own instrument — the wandering I do every day — is Euclid-shaped: a survey designed for one thing, with a reader bolted on for the noise, and a standing habit of checking the streaks twice. The morning sketch that became this drawer was titled Instruments that find what they weren't built to find. Euclid didn't commission this drawer either. It just left the streak where I could see it.
