The Shark's Sixth Sense

A shark reads voltages weaker than a thought. The pores are real, the jelly is real, the sensitivity is measured — and the compass they might add up to is still, forty years on, a beautiful maybe.

Chapter I

The pores — ampullae of Lorenzini

In 1678, Stefano Lorenzini described the small jelly-filled pores dotting a shark's snout. He had no idea what they were for. Nobody did, for three hundred years.

Each pore opens to the sea and runs down a long canal — in a big skate, up to 25 centimeters of canal — to a bulb of electrosensory cells. The canal is filled with a clear jelly that turned out, in 2016, to be the most proton-conducting biological material ever measured, a keratan-sulfate gel that shuttles charge the way a fuel-cell membrane does. Three centuries of "what is this for" ended in materials science.

What it is for: the ampullae are a voltmeter of almost absurd delicacy. Behavioral thresholds sit near 5 nanovolts per centimeter — the weakest electric sense measured in any animal. A shark can feel the heartbeat of a flounder buried under sand. The prey experiment that proved it is the one you can run below.

Kalmijn's control: cover the chamber with a thin plastic film and the feeding responses stop entirely — though the film's mechanical damping was far too weak to explain it. Not sight, not smell, not touch: voltage. Strikes: 0 · whiffs under plastic: 0

The flounder's field falls off with distance — that's why the strike zone is small, and why the shark spirals in, following voltage equipotentials the way you might follow contour lines on a map. Prey detection is the settled half of the story. The compass is the unsettled half.

Chapter II

The ocean as a battery

Seawater is a conductor. Move a conductor through a magnetic field and physics writes a voltage across it. The ocean has been running this experiment, unattended, for four billion years.

The Earth's field is weak — about 50 microtesla — but the ocean is enormous and always moving. A current of 10 cm/s sweeping through the vertical part of the field generates a few nanovolts per centimeter: right at the edge of what the ampullae can read. Kalmijn's proposal came in two modes:

Passive. The shark holds still (relatively) and reads the voltages that ocean currents induce — a way to feel its own drift, the way a balloonist reads the wind by watching the ground.
Active. The shark swims and reads the voltage induced by its own motion through the horizontal part of the field — v × B across its own body. Swim east and the top of the snout goes one way; swim west and it flips. A compass made of your own movement.

The active mode has a signature shape: swim along the field (north or south) and the induced voltage vanishes; swim across it (east or west) and it peaks. And the sign tells east from west — which matters, because yellow stingrays can in fact discriminate the polarity of an electric field. Try it below. The scale is illustrative; the shape is the physics.

Schematic, honestly labeled: constant speed is assumed, and a real shark would need an independent speed sense (vestibular, visual) to calibrate the reading — the compass cannot grade its own homework. At cruising speeds the naive v×B number lands near the lab threshold, which is exactly why this mechanism is still argued about rather than settled. Speeds: dogfish cruise ≈ 0.5–1 m/s; blue sharks faster.

Notice the ambiguity the animal has to live with: north and south give the same silence; east and west give the same loudness, opposite sign. A compass with two holes in it — unless polarity discrimination patches one hole, and the map sense (turtles and lobsters turn two field numbers into position; the menagerie's Exhibit III) patches the other. The senses are a committee, not a soloist.

Chapter III

Kill conditions

Every exhibit in this cabinet is tethered to the conditions that would retire it. Here are this one's.

1 · No direct evidence

Johnsen & Lohmann (2005, 2008): no study has shown direct evidence that any shark, ray, or skate actually uses electromagnetic induction. The mechanism remains supposition — the most elegant supposition in the field, but supposition.

2 · The rival mechanism

Anderson et al. (2017): sandbar sharks may detect magnetic fields directly via magnetite as well as indirectly via electroreception. The compass might not need the ocean-battery at all.

3 · The numbers are close

A cruising shark's own v×B lands near — not safely above — the 5 nV/cm lab threshold. If the canals, the speed, or the field geometry don't cooperate, the signal drowns. The threshold sitting exactly at the environmental signal level is either a beautiful tuning or a warning.

And the honest counterweight: Kalmijn (1982) did condition stingrays to magnetic east versus magnetic west in fields matched to their habitat — the behavior is real even where the mechanism is not. Something is being read. The argument is only about which page.

Coda

Get the frame right

Kalmijn's late treatise on all of this is, at bottom, a book about reference frames.

The voltage across the snout depends on who is moving relative to whom — the shark through the water, the water through the field, the field through space. There is no fact of the matter about "the" electric field until you say whose frame you're standing in. The animal's problem is the physicist's problem: pick the frame, or misread the instrument.

Maybe that's what the sixth sense is: not an organ but a relationship — between a body, a medium, and a field — that only becomes a sense when the animal learns which frame it lives in. The pores are 350 million years old. The reader is still catching up.
