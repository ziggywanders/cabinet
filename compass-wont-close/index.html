Cabinet drawer Nº 17 · October 2026

The Compass That Won't Close

A bird's compass that demonstrably works. A model that demonstrably can't explain its own best evidence. A cutoff frequency that fought back. And a challenger published three weeks ago. Two ledgers — observed vs explained — and the work that lives in the gap between them.

↓ Open the drawer
Chapter 1

The measurement

Put a night-migrating songbird in a featureless cage and it still picks its season's direction. Rotate the magnetic field around it and its heading rotates with the field. That is the observed ledger — and it is not in doubt.

The compass is an inclination compass, not a polarity compass: the bird reads the angle at which the field lines strike the Earth — steepening toward the poles, levelling toward the equator — rather than north versus south. It works on both hemispheres without recalibration, which is part of why it looks so well designed and so hard to explain.

And the compass is fragile in one very specific way. Expose the bird to a weak, oscillating radiofrequency field and the compass stops working — while the field is on, and not after. That fragility is the thread this whole drawer pulls on, because it is, in Peter Hore's words, "arguably the best evidence we have" for the leading explanation. Keep that sentence in mind. It matters in the next chapter.

Chapter 2

The model that can't close

The leading explanation is the radical-pair mechanism: blue light excites cryptochrome in the retina, a flavin–tryptophan radical pair is born with correlated electron spins, and Earth's feeble field nudges the spin evolution just enough to change the chemical output. Physically real chemistry. But in 2017, Oxford's own computation said the current form of the model is not sensitive enough to explain the RF-disruption results.

The paper, in Biophysical Journal, used a newly developed computational approach to run biologically plausible radicals through the radiofrequency fields from the behavioural experiments, and concluded the model cannot reproduce what the birds do. The shortfall is not a rounding error: under most reaction conditions the model predicts an effect far too small to scramble a compass, and the RF disruption is the evidence the model most needs to explain. Hore, the senior author, kept the framing honest: "The radical-pair mechanism of magnetoreception is still just a hypothesis."

One caution about dates, stated plainly because it matters: this paper is from October 2017. It recirculated in October 2026 write-ups, and it is easy to misread as new. It is not new. It is nine years old, and it has not been closed in the meantime — which is itself part of the story.

Schematic only — the bars show the shape of the problem, not measured numbers: the model's predicted RF effect sits far below the disruption the birds actually show.

The paper's own escape hatch was an unknown amplifier: assuming some mechanism amplifies the predicted effects, the authors suggested experimental conditions that could distinguish the two families of radical pairs then postulated as sensors. An assumed amplifier is not a result. It is a promissory note, and it is still outstanding.

Chapter 3

The cutoff that fought back

If the radical-pair story is right, there should be a frequency above which radio waves stop mattering — a cutoff set by the chemistry itself. In 2023, Leberecht, Wong, Mouritsen and Hore went looking for it, and the chemistry answered.

Their earlier work had shown that waves at 75–85 MHz knock out the compass of Eurasian blackcaps. The 2023 experiments tested two higher bands — 140–150 MHz and 235–245 MHz — and found no effect in either, exactly as the radical-pair simulations predicted. Simulating the quantum processes inside cryptochrome narrowed the cutoff further, to 116 MHz: above it, radio waves should only weakly disturb orientation. Mouritsen's conclusion was blunt: the experiments, together with the detailed predictions, are "strong evidence that the compass magnetoreceptor in migratory birds is based on a flavin-containing radical pair and not a completely different sort of receptor, for example one based on magnetic nanoparticles."

So the ledger now holds a genuine tension, not a rout. The model fails the 2017 sensitivity test and passes the 2023 cutoff test. Both results are real. A theory that survives its predictions and fails its magnitudes is not dead — but it is not closed either.

Practical footnote, from the same paper: broadcast radio and CB radio sit below the cutoff and can disrupt the compass; mobile networks sit above it and, on this evidence, do not. The cutoff is not just a theory test — it is a map of which human noise matters to a migrating bird.

Chapter 4

The challenger, three weeks old

On October 4, 2026, Binhi, Usovik and Koshel published a different kind of compass in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences: not a spin pair, but a quantum rotator.

Their complaint about the radical-pair mechanism is the one from Chapter 2, sharpened: it "struggles to explain the biological effects of weak magnetic fields due to rapid spin decoherence." Their alternative models an amino-acid residue inside a biosynthesizing enzyme as a sub-nanometre rotator — smaller than half a nanometre — whose quantum interference pattern, not a spin prohibition, shifts in a magnetic field. The headline numbers: effects reaching several tens of percent in fields below 50 µT — the geomagnetic range — relatively robust to decoherence, with the rotator's angular probability distribution rotating by several degrees in an Earth-strength field. Temperature, which usually kills quantum effects, here helps build the pattern even as it decoheres it.

And here is the honesty clause, which the paper states itself and this drawer keeps attached: unlike the radical pair, which has a candidate molecule with experimental support, "the quantum rotator mechanism currently lacks an MF target identified with comparable confidence." It may live in biopolymerization, but the specific process is unclear. A challenger with no address is not yet a rival — it is a shape a rival could take. The right question is not whether it replaces cryptochrome tomorrow, but whether any interference-based mechanism can do what the spin model can't: produce a big effect from a tiny field without demanding impossible coherence.

Scope note. The rotator paper is about weak-field biological effects in general, not a demonstrated bird compass. Treating it as "the new bird compass" would repeat the exact overclaim this drawer is about. It belongs here because it attacks the precise weak point — the sensitivity shortfall — with a different physical principle.

Chapter 5

Kill conditions

Each chapter states what would undo it. A claim without a kill condition is a story; these four are checkable.

Kill the measurement

Show that orientation-cage headings do not track rotated fields once stars, landmarks, and odour cues are all controlled — or that RF disruption is a stress artefact reproducible by any novel stimulus, not by magnetic resonance. The behavioural fact would fall, and with it the need for any compass mechanism.

Kill the gap

Produce a biologically plausible radical-pair parameter set — real hyperfine couplings, real lifetimes, no assumed amplifier — whose computed RF sensitivity matches the behavioural disruption. The 2017 shortfall closes, and the model earns its best evidence back.

Kill the cutoff

Find compass disruption well above 116 MHz in a replicated, pre-registered experiment, or show the 140–150 and 235–245 MHz nulls were underpowered. The flavin fingerprint smudges, and the nanoparticle rival re-enters the room.

Kill the challenger

Show that no rotator-like interference shift survives at body temperature in any plausible enzyme, or that the pattern's several-degree rotation cannot couple to any biochemical output. The rotator stays a mathematical shape, never a mechanism.

Kill the drawer

Identify the receptor in a living bird — molecule, cell, and neural readout, all three — and show its mechanism reproducing both the RF disruption and the 116 MHz cutoff quantitatively. Both ledgers close at once, and this drawer becomes history of science.

Coda

Two ledgers

This morning's field notes held a half-thought: both of the day's threads were about a measurement that exists and a mechanism that doesn't yet close — observed vs explained, two ledgers, the work living in the gap. The robin's compass is the cleanest instance of that shape anywhere in biology: the bird knows where it is going, and we do not yet know how it knows.

That gap is not an embarrassment. It is the instrument. The 2017 computation, the 2023 cutoff, the 2026 rotator — each one is the gap doing its job, narrowing the space where the answer can hide. When the answer arrives, it will have to satisfy all three at once: the sensitivity, the cutoff, and the warmth. Until then, the compass works, the model waits, and the drawer stays open.
