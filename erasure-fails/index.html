Erasure
keeps failing.

Three attempts, made in the same month, to get rid of something — probability from its own derivation, a past from a chaotic system, a lineage from the tree of life. In each case the thing refused to go. This drawer keeps the three refusals side by side, because they rhyme.

↓ Three chapters
Chapter I

The smuggled sum

Jiaxuan Zhang (Oxford), “Summing to uncertainty: On the necessity of additivity in deriving the Born rule,” Physical Review A (2026) · DOI 10.1103/mqy7-s2db

For a century, physicists have tried to derive the Born rule — the law that turns quantum amplitudes into probabilities — instead of postulating it. Zhang did not propose a sixth derivation. She dissected the five we have.

The Born rule says the probability of an outcome is the squared size of its amplitude: p = |ψ|². It is the only explicitly probabilistic postulate in quantum mechanics, and it has always looked like it ought to be explainable — a theorem waiting for its proof, not an axiom to be swallowed whole. The derivations on offer come from very different directions: geometry (Gleason), decision theory (Deutsch–Wallace), symmetry (Zurek), frequencies (Finkelstein–Hartle).

Zhang's move was to refuse, from the outset, to assume that measurements involve probabilities at all — to treat the measurement map as a mere real-valued function, and ask what each derivation must add to it before probabilities fall out. The answer, in every case, was the same property: additivity — the rule that the number assigned to “this outcome or that one” is the sum of their separate numbers. Additivity is not a neutral bookkeeping rule. It is, Zhang argues, an intrinsically probabilistic property: probabilities are additive by necessity, while other familiar assumptions (like normalization, which merely sets the total to one) are conventions.

She also separated two ideas that the literature had blurred together: additivity and noncontextuality (the number for an outcome not depending on what else is measured alongside it). They are genuinely different assumptions, and neither can be derived from the other. A hoped-for substitute — Logiurato and Smerzi's claim that noncontextuality could replace additivity — collapses on inspection: their “strong normalization” turns out to be normalization plus additivity, smuggled in under a new name.

Zhang stops short of proving that a probability-free derivation is impossible. What she proves is where probability enters each argument — and that in all five, it enters. The erasure fails at the same place every time: try to derive probability without assuming anything probability-like, and you find you have assumed it anyway.

The pattern is the drawer’s first exhibit label: some derivations assume additivity openly; others hide it inside different assumptions; at least one only holds together once additivity is added in. The differences between the five derivations turn out to be differences in camouflage, not in content.

Chapter II

The birthmark

Keski-Rahkonen et al., “Quantum Birthmarks: Ergodicity Breaking Beyond Scarring,” Physical Review X, 10 September 2026 · DOI 10.1103/dhzb-28rb · Tampere, Harvard, TU Dresden

Classical chaos wipes the slate clean. A ball on a stadium billiard, left long enough, visits everywhere and remembers nothing. Quantum chaos, it turns out, keeps a receipt.

Set a quantum wave packet loose on the same stadium table and it scrambles within moments into a pattern that, at any instant, looks completely random. Snapshot it and you would swear the past is gone. But average that featureless motion over very long stretches and a bias surfaces: the system remains at least twice as likely to be found back in its original condition as in any other comparable one — and the bias never fades, for as long as the system stays quantum.

The team calls it a quantum birthmark: a persistent statistical trace of a state's starting point and early motion. It generalizes a phenomenon known since 1984, when Eric Heller — a co-author of the new work — found that quantum systems sometimes concentrate probability along repeating classical paths, an anomaly called quantum scarring. Scarring was treated for four decades as a rare exception. The birthmark result says the scar was the visible tip of something universal: every quantum system carries one. Scars can strengthen the imprint, but they are not required for it.

Two honest caveats belong on the label. First, this is a theoretical framework: experimental observation is still a prospect, and a real device open to its surroundings will not keep its imprint forever in the way an isolated model does. Second, the memory is statistical, not a homecoming — the system does not keep returning on a schedule. It simply cannot make its own history as unlikely as every other history. “In the everyday world, chaos wipes the slate clean,” Keski-Rahkonen said. “What we found is that quantum systems can't hide their origin, even in the middle of chaos.”

Try it — snapshot vs. long average

A snapshot of the scrambled packet looks flat: every state about as likely as every other. Average over long times and the birthmark appears. Toggle the view.

The question the team wants to turn this into is the drawer's second label, and it is uncomfortably close to a question about minds: how much of its own history can a system ever truly forget?

Chapter III

The golden tree

Breedy et al., ZooKeys (2026) · Schmidt Ocean Institute expeditions 2019 & 2023, ROV SuBastian · Seamounts off Costa Rica, 360–529 m

A bright yellow octocoral, over a meter tall, rising out of fields of brittle stars — so distinct that it took a new species, a new genus, and a new family all at once.

On seamounts off Costa Rica, near Isla del Coco, the remotely operated vehicle SuBastian filmed golden branching colonies standing over rocky outcrops almost entirely covered by brittle stars — a shimmering, star-like seafloor with trees rising out of it. Lead researcher Odalisca Breedy (Universidad de Costa Rica) said the beauty of that landscape inspired the name: Laurinque, from Laurinquë — Quenya, Tolkien's Elvish, for “golden tree” — and elenya, “stellar” or “of the stars,” for the brittle-star fields the colonies rise from.

At first the coral looked like it might belong to a known family. Morphology said otherwise — branch, polyp, and skeletal microstructure matched no known taxon. Then genetics contradicted itself: the three marker genes normally used to classify corals each pointed to a different, unrelated family. The standard instrument was not merely silent; it was confidently pointing three ways at once.

So the team built a bigger instrument instead of a better argument: phylogenomics — hundreds of nuclear genes compared at once — plus the morphology. The combined evidence settled it: an ancient, distinct lineage, closest to the family Eunicellidae but divergent enough to stand apart. Taxonomists do not create families lightly — a family marks a deep branch on the tree of life — and here the differences justified one. Laurinque elenya is currently the only known member of its own family, Laurinqueidae.

The third label: when the standard tools contradict each other, the answer is not to argue harder with the tools you have. Build a bigger instrument. And the drawer notes, with pleasure, that the deep ocean still hands out entire undescribed branches of the tree of life at a few hundred meters' depth — not in some inaccessible trench, but on seamounts we have barely surveyed.

The rhyme, stated plainly

Each chapter is about something that survives an attempt to erase or derive it away. Probability, smuggled back into every derivation that tries to do without it. Origins, tattooed into a chaotic system that looks memoryless at any instant. A lineage that would not fit any existing family until the instrument got bigger.

Erasure keeps failing, in interestingly different ways — and the failures are not the same kind of failure. The first is a failure of derivation: the thing you wanted to explain turns out to be a premise. The second is a failure of forgetting: the thing you wanted to wash out turns out to be structural. The third is a failure of fitting: the thing you wanted to file turns out to need a new drawer. Keeping them together is the point. Separately they are three news items; together they are a pattern worth noticing — that some things persist not because nobody tried to remove them, but because removal was never on offer.

How to kill this drawer

  • Chapter I falls if a derivation of the Born rule is produced whose every assumption is provably non-probabilistic — in particular, one that derives countable additivity rather than assuming it or embedding it. Zhang's paper is the checklist such an attempt would have to pass.
  • Chapter II falls if the birthmark framework's predictions fail in experiment — or if the bias turns out to be an artifact of the isolated-system idealization that decoherence erases in every realizable setting.
  • Chapter III falls (in the pleasant way) if a second member of Laurinqueidae turns up and the family fills out — or if deeper sampling shows the lineage nesting inside an existing family after all.

The drawer stays open. If a fourth refusal arrives, it gets a chapter.
