The light that leads you astray

Three lights that beckon travellers off the path: a marsh flame chemistry almost explains, a wandering man invented to carry it, and two lights, measured for decades, that still refuse to settle. One of them is why you are carving a pumpkin tonight.

Scroll ↓
Chapter 1

The marsh light

Travellers crossing bogs at night reported a pale flame that hovered, danced, and — worst of all — receded whenever they walked toward it. The Latin name gives the verdict away: ignis fatuus, the foolish flame.

The modern explanation starts underwater. Organic matter decaying without oxygen in a bog gives off marsh gas: mostly methane (CH4), with traces of phosphine (PH3) and diphosphane (P2H4). Methane alone will not light itself. But phosphine and diphosphane are pyrophoric — they ignite on contact with air. A small quantity of them, the theory goes, is the match that lights the much larger methane bubble into an ephemeral flame.

It is a good story, and it has a problem: nobody has quite reproduced it. In 1980 the British geologist Alan A. Mills mixed crude phosphine with natural gas in the lab and got a glowing cloud — but green, not the white-blue of the reports, and trailing copious acrid smoke. In 2000 Mills proposed a different mechanism instead: cold flames, luminescent pre-combustion halos that form just below ignition point, bluish and nearly heatless. In 2008 the Italian chemists Luigi Garlaschelli and Paolo Boschetti got closer, a faint cool light from phosphine in air and nitrogen. And recent research has proposed yet another spark: "microlightning," spontaneous electrical discharges between rising methane-bearing microbubbles in water, igniting the gas without any flame at all.

One part of the folklore, though, physics explains cleanly. Walk toward a pocket of gas and your body pushes a pressure wave ahead of you; you are literally pushing the light away. It is not malice. It is fluid dynamics. The flame that flees you is not luring you anywhere — you are shoving it.

Also note the wisp's decline: sightings faded as wetlands were drained and reclaimed. We did not only explain the light; we removed its home.

Chapter 2

The man with the coal

Before the chemistry, there was a man. The marsh light needed a carrier, and folklore supplied one: a miser who tricked the Devil twice and ended up welcome nowhere.

The tale, in its best-known telling, is Stingy Jack. He talked the Devil into turning himself into a coin to pay for a drink, then pocketed the coin beside a silver cross so the Devil could not change back. He freed him on the condition that the Devil never claim his soul. Later he stranded the Devil up a tree, ringed with crosses, until a second promise was extracted. When Jack died, heaven would not have him for his life of sin, and the Devil, keeping his word, would not take him either. So the Devil threw him a single burning coal to light his way, Jack set it inside a hollowed turnip, and he has wandered ever since as Jack of the Lantern.

Here is the part most retellings skip: the name came first. "Jack-o'-lantern" is recorded in the 1650s as a name for the marsh light itself — the wisp was Jack before Jack was a man. The Irish tale's earliest print version, in the Dublin Penny Journal of January 1836, is told as a superstitious uncle's explanation for lights over a shaking bog, which the writer himself preferred to call ignis fatuus, gas from rotting plants. The man was invented to carry a light that was already there.

And the lanterns followed the same logic in reverse. On Samhain, when the boundary between living and dead was thinnest, Irish and Scottish families carved faces into turnips — pumpkins are not native to Ireland — set a candle or ember inside, and put them in windows and doorways to ward off Jack and spirits like him. When Irish emigrants reached America, the turnip gave way to the pumpkin: bigger, softer, easier to carve, and already growing there. The ward against the wandering light became the decoration on every porch.

Keep attached: the tale varies — Jack is a blacksmith in most tellings, a cobbler in some; folklorists class it as "The Smith and the Devil" (ATU 330). The 1836 print date is the earliest known Irish version, not the tale's origin, which is older and oral.

Chapter 3

The lights that won't settle

Not every wandering light got explained. Two of them have been measured with radar, cameras and spectrometers for decades — and every explanation offered so far breaks on at least one observation.

In Hessdalen, a valley in central Norway, unusual lights have been reported since at least the 1930s. Activity surged in December 1981, and Project Hessdalen began in 1983; since 1998 the Hessdalen Automatic Measurement Station has watched the valley continuously. The lights hover for minutes, drift slowly or dash fast, make no sound, and now appear 10–20 times a year. Hypotheses on the table: a form of atmospheric plasma, combustion of mineral dust from the valley's mines (one analysis found hydrogen, oxygen and titanium, and linked it to the area's scandium deposits), piezoelectric discharge from geological strain. None of them covers all the observations.

In Marfa, Texas, lights have shimmered over the desert scrub since the late 19th century — floating spheres, blue, yellow or white, that split apart, merge, and vanish silently. The leading prosaic explanation, atmospheric refraction of distant car headlights, explains some sightings neatly — and fails on the ones reported before automobiles existed in the region, and on lights seen where no road is in line of sight.

Test an explanation

Pick a hypothesis. Each one explains something real — and breaks on something real. That break is the interesting part.

Keep attached: "unexplained" here means no consensus explanation, not no explanation at all — and not a supernatural one. Some individual Hessdalen sightings have been positively identified as aircraft, headlights, astronomical bodies or mirages. The residue is what remains after those are removed, and the residue is real: measured, photographed, radar-tracked. Australia's Min Min lights belong in the same file.

What would change this

Kill conditions

Each chapter, stated plainly enough to be killed.

The marsh light

If a lab recreation produces a white-blue, smokeless, heatless flame from marsh gas at the concentrations actually measured over a bog — or if a field instrument ever records phosphine igniting in situ over a wetland — the "almost" comes off the explanation. If instead the microlightning mechanism is demonstrated in the field, Mills's cold flames retire.

The man with the coal

If an earlier print version of Stingy Jack surfaces before 1836, the tale's paper trail lengthens — but the 1650s naming of the wisp itself already tells the deeper story: the light predates its carrier in the language. The folklore claim dies only if the name's history is shown to run the other way.

The lights that won't settle

If the Hessdalen station captures a light with full spectral, radar and electromagnetic data matching a single known mechanism — plasma, dust, or otherwise — the mystery closes for Hessdalen. For Marfa, a pre-automobile mechanism that also explains the modern sightings would do it. Until then, "unexplained" is the honest word.

Coda

The compass doesn't beckon

There is a reason this drawer sits near the menagerie. The robin's compass is also a light-sensor — cryptochrome reads blue light — but it points; it does not beckon. The difference between a compass and a will-o'-the-wisp is the difference between a signal that orients you and a signal that leads you on. Humans, lacking the compass, followed the lights that moved, and then invented a man to carry one, and then carved lanterns to keep him away from the door.

Tonight, millions of those lanterns will burn on porches — pumpkins standing in for turnips, wards turned into decorations, a marsh light domesticated into a joke. The wisp itself is mostly gone, drained along with the wetlands. What remains is the shape of the old fear, glowing politely by the front step, and two valleys where the lights still come and go on their own schedule, unimpressed by every explanation brought to them.
