The Quantum Ladder

For a century, quantum mechanics has been climbing toward the world you can hold — one experiment, one rung, one order of magnitude at a time. This is the climb so far. And the next rung might finally reach gravity.

↓ climb
Prologue

Why does the world look classical?

Nobody disputes that electrons interfere with themselves. The question is why nothing bigger seems to — and whether that's a law of nature or a failure of engineering.

In 1935 Erwin Schrödinger wrote down a thought experiment to show the absurdity of taking quantum theory literally at scale: a cat in a box, its fate entangled with a single decaying atom, both dead and alive until someone looks. He meant it as a reductio. Ninety years later it is a research program.

There are two families of answers. The decoherence family says the quantum-ness never goes away — it leaks. Every collision with air, every photon bouncing off, entangles the object with its environment and scrambles the interference pattern faster than any experiment can read it. Bigger objects couple to more environment; the ladder is an engineering problem. The collapse family — Ghirardi–Rimini–Weber, continuous spontaneous localization, Diósi–Penrose — says the theory itself changes at scale: superpositions of sufficiently massive objects collapse on their own, with a rate that grows with mass. On this view the ladder has a top, and the top is a discovery.

Every rung on this ladder tests both at once. A successful rung is an engineering triumph and a tightening of the noose around collapse models. That's why the ladder matters: it is the only instrument we have for finding out whether quantum mechanics has an edge — and the instrument is built by climbing.

Chapter one

The rungs

Seven experiments, ninety-nine years, roughly twenty-six orders of magnitude in mass. Drag your eye along the bottom; click a marker to visit its rung.

Rung 0 · The base — 1927

Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer (and, independently, George Thomson) fire electrons at a nickel crystal and watch them diffract. Matter has a wavelength — de Broglie's 1923 prediction, confirmed. Everything above this rung is the question: how far up does the wave survive?

Kill conditionAlready settled: no one has ever repeated electron diffraction and found the fringes missing. The base holds.
720 amu · 60 atoms

Rung 1 · The buckyball — Vienna, 1999

Markus Arndt, Anton Zeilinger and colleagues send C60 — a soccer-ball molecule a nanometer across, big enough to see under a microscope — through a diffraction grating and watch it interfere with itself. The molecule's de Broglie wavelength is 2.5 picometers: four hundred times smaller than the molecule itself. Their own paper called C60 "almost a classical body," because of its many internal degrees of freedom and their couplings to the environment — which is precisely what made the result matter: decoherence had to be beaten, and was.

Kill conditionWould have failed if the molecules' internal heat or the grating's van der Waals forces washed out the fringes. The fringes arrived anyway — and the interference pattern set the template for everything that followed.
microns across · billions of atoms

Rung 2 · The quantum drumbeat — UCSB, 2010

Andrew Cleland and John Martinis's group builds a 6 GHz piezoelectric mechanical resonator and does the opposite of what everyone expected: instead of laser-cooling a slow oscillator, they make the oscillator so fast that a dilution refrigerator drops it straight into its quantum ground state. Then, coupling it to a superconducting qubit, they create single phonons — individual quanta of vibration — in a manufactured object you could almost see. The first deliberate quantum control of a mechanical thing.

Kill conditionAny uncontrolled thermal phonon would have shown up as a population the qubit couldn't account for. The resonator sat at the bottom rung of its energy ladder, empty, and the group put it there one quantum at a time.
millimeter-scale · ~10 mg

Rung 3 · Entangled diamonds, room temperature — Oxford, 2011

Katherine Lee and colleagues link two millimeter-scale diamond chips with a single shared phonon — the crystals' vibrational modes entangled at room temperature, in broad daylight, on a bench. The entanglement is fleeting (picoseconds), but the point stands: the quantum effect doesn't need the cold, and it doesn't need the small. Two objects you could pick up with tweezers, briefly, were one system.

Kill conditionThermal noise at 300 K should drown any shared quantum state instantly. The team read the entanglement out with ultrafast pulses faster than the noise — speed as the countermeasure to scale.
25,000 amu · ~2,000 atoms

Rung 4 · Two thousand atoms in two places — Vienna & Basel, 2019

Yaakov Fein, Markus Arndt and colleagues push matter-wave interferometry to molecules of over 25 kDa — hulking porphyrin derivatives, some 25,000 times the mass of hydrogen, held in superposition for more than 7 milliseconds inside a two-meter interferometer. The beamline was so large the team had to correct for gravity and the rotation of the Earth. The experiment set new interferometric bounds on spontaneous-collapse models and what the authors call an order-of-magnitude increase in "macroscopicity" — a formal score for how hard a given experiment squeezes the alternatives to quantum mechanics.

Kill conditionThis rung is explicitly a noose: if any collapse model were right at the scale it predicts, the fringes would have been gone. They weren't. Every future model now has to survive Vienna's two-meter machine.
~10¹³ amu · ~10¹² atoms each

Rung 5 · The trillion-atom drums — NIST, 2021

John Teufel's group at NIST entangles two aluminum drumheads — each about a trillion atoms, the width of a human hair — and measures the entanglement directly, deterministically, reading motions a quadrillionth of a meter across with radar-like microwaves. Looked at one drum alone, each is just hot; looked at together, their random motions are correlated in a way no classical system can fake. The same year, a parallel Aalto experiment used two entangled drums to sidestep Heisenberg's back-action — measuring a quantum system by making the measurement's disturbance cancel itself.

Kill conditionThe entanglement claim would die if the correlations could be explained by a shared thermal bath. The team measured during separated phases inside a refrigerator — the correlations survived, the classical alibi didn't.
100 mg · ~10²³ amu · centimeter-scale

Rung 6 · Spin moves a diamond you can hold — OIST, 2026

At the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology, Anshuman Nayak and Jason Twamley build a hybrid lever: a 100-milligram diamond, studded with billions of nitrogen-vacancy centers — each an unpaired electron, a tiny controllable quantum magnet — hung on a carbon rod that rises through magnetic shielding to a diamagnetically levitated graphite plate carrying a mirror. A 50-milliwatt green laser pulse polarizes the electron spins; the changing spin state changes the diamond's magnetic moment; the diamond is pushed down toward a magnet below. An interferometer reads the plate's motion with picometer precision. Electron spin alone, visibly shifting a centimeter-wide object — eight to nine orders of magnitude more massive than the previous state of the art in spin-mechanical experiments, and the first time a quantum effect has been seen directly pushing around an object subject to gravity.

The move to admire: the field's standing strategy was to levitate something tiny and climb upward toward gravity's regime. This team went large-to-small instead — diamagnetic levitation (the same trick behind maglev trains) holds the centimeter scale, and the quantum came down to meet it.

Kill conditionThe obvious impostor is heat: a 50 mW laser is a small heater, and heat moves things too. The experiment's control is a pulse that delivers the same energy without flipping the spins — same heat, no push. Try the control below.

Chapter three

The next rung

Jason Twamley's own framing: one more order of magnitude, and the experiment reaches "Schrödinger's cat in real life."

"We've shown a large classical response from a small quantum effect. We're pushing the bar from nanometers to centimeters. All we need is another order of magnitude, and we can finally observe Schrödinger's cat in real life." — Jason Twamley, OIST, on the 2026 result
Logarithmic mass scale — where the ladder stands, and how far the cat sits.

What's up there is not just a bigger diamond. At that scale sit the experiments that could settle the oldest question in the field: is gravity quantum? Proposals like Bose–et al.'s 2017 scheme would entangle two masses through their mutual gravitational attraction — if the entanglement appears, gravity must be a quantum mediator; no classical gravity can fake it. The OIST lever is the on-ramp: a proven way to put quantum state information into, and read quantum-scale motion out of, an object you can hold.

And here's the quiet part of the program, the part that comes free: the philosophy experiment is also a sensor. A centimeter-scale object whose motion is read at picometer precision, driven by quantum spins, is a force detector. The same lever that chases Schrödinger's cat is natively sensitive to the faintest nudges imaginable — the territory of dark matter candidates and gravitational waves. The instrument for the deepest question and the instrument for the most practical sensing are the same machine. Build the ladder, get the telescope.

Chapter four

What would end the climb

A ladder with no top is just a road. This one has two candidate ceilings — and finding either would be the biggest discovery of all.

The practical ceiling: decoherence

Every rung fights the environment. Air molecules, blackbody photons, seismic rumble — each entangles with the quantum object and smears the pattern. Beating it is an engineering war fought with vacuum chambers, cryostats, magnetic shielding, and speed. So far engineering keeps winning. But it gets harder with every order of magnitude, and it gets harder faster than the mass grows.

The theoretical ceiling: collapse

Spontaneous-collapse models predict a genuine breakdown: above some mass and some timescale, superpositions simply don't survive, no matter how perfect the vacuum. Each successful rung redraws that boundary upward and tightens the parameters the models are allowed. The day a rung fails — the fringes refuse to appear despite perfect engineering — is the day quantum mechanics shows its edge. That failure would be a Nobel.

The mirror ceiling: gravity

Diósi–Penrose and friends tie collapse to gravity itself: a superposition of a massive object is a superposition of spacetimes, and spacetime refuses. This is why the OIST result matters beyond its size — it's the first rung standing inside gravity's regime rather than climbing toward it. The ladder and the theory of gravity are now in the same room.

The honest version of the story, the one the menagerie pages tell too: "maybe" belongs in every sentence. The ladder might top out at a fundamental scale. It might not top out at all. Nobody has earned the right to claim either yet — which is exactly what makes the next rung worth building.
