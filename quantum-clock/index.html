The clock that disagrees with itself

Relativity says a moving clock runs slow. Quantum mechanics says a clock's motion can be in superposition. Put them together and one clock should tick faster and slower at once — and a trapped ion, squeezed hard enough, should be able to show it.

Scroll ↓
Chapter 1

The twins, quantum

The twin paradox is the oldest story relativity tells: send one twin on a fast round trip and she comes home younger than the one who stayed. It was first directly observed in 1971, when Hafele and Keating flew atomic clocks around the world and brought them home disagreeing with the clocks left on the ground.

The effect is real but small. A clock moving at 10 metres per second — a brisk run — would have to keep it up for 57 million years to fall behind by a single second. Every measurement of time dilation so far can be explained the same way: the clock evolves with respect to one classical proper time, a single parameter, and a quantum sensor merely reads it.

But there is a more counterintuitive version, and it has been on the books since 2011, when Zych, Costa, Pikovski and Brukner asked the question plainly: can a single clock experience two different times in quantum superposition — become both younger and older at once? Their answer was yes in principle, and the signature would be a loss of interferometric visibility: the clock's internal evolution entangles with its own motion, and the fringes fade by exactly the amount the entanglement costs.

And the clock you carry changes the time you measure in a second way. Zambianco and Perche (2026) built a clock out of the vacuum-decay probability of a finite-sized quantum system and found its proper time depends on the clock's microscopic properties — on how it samples vacuum fluctuations along its path. In the long-time regime it reduces to ordinary special-relativistic proper time. But in a microscopic twin-paradox scenario, two clocks on the same trajectory can disagree — because of what they are made of. The trajectory is not the whole story. The instrument is part of the time.

Three regimes of proper time

Classical: the clock moves on one trajectory and evolves by one proper time τ — a parameter. Semiclassical: the clock is quantum but its motion is treated classically, evolving by the average ⟨τ⟩ — every measurement so far lives here. Quantum: proper time itself becomes an operator τ̂, the clock's evolution entangles with its motion, and the fringes lose visibility. The 2026 paper says the third regime is now within reach.

Why now, and why an ion

Optical ion clocks are the most precise instruments ever built. The current NIST aluminium-ion clock — one 27Al+ ion doing the timekeeping, a co-trapped 25Mg+ ion doing the cooling and readout — carries a fractional systematic uncertainty of 5.5 × 10−19 (Marshall et al., 2025). That is the instrument the prediction is aimed at: the effect it predicts is bigger than the clock's own error bar, and the squeezing it needs has already been demonstrated in ion traps.

Chapter 2

Squeeze the clock

Sorci, Foo, Leibfried, Sanner and Pikovski (Physical Review Letters, April 20, 2026) did the calculation properly: a Hamiltonian in which the clock's rest energy includes its internal energy, so the clock and its motion evolve together — and proper time becomes an operator, τ̂, built out of the ion's position and momentum.

Cool the ion to its motional ground state and something already happens: the vacuum energy of the trap shifts the clock by about 5 × 10−19 — a vacuum-induced second-order Doppler shift (vSODS), small enough to hide under the clock's own uncertainty. But squeeze the motion — prepare a motional squeezed state, stretching the position uncertainty while pinching the momentum, or the reverse — and two things grow together: the clock's frequency shifts by an amount that scales as cosh(2r), and the Ramsey fringes lose visibility as the clock entangles with its own motion. At squeezing parameter r = 2.26, already demonstrated in ion traps, in a 20 MHz trap with one second of free evolution, the paper predicts a visibility of V ≃ 0.93 — a 7% fade — and a squeezing-induced frequency shift of Δν/ν ≃ 3.8 × 10−17.

That shift is about 69 times the aluminium clock's systematic uncertainty. The visibility drop is the hallmark: a frequency shift alone could be a systematic error, but a fringe that fades by exactly the amount the entanglement predicts — and returns when the squeezing is switched off — is the clock disagreeing with itself, on the record.

The Ramsey fringes — squeeze and watch them fade

Drag the squeezing up. The amber curve is the unsqueezed clock (full visibility); the teal curve is the same clock with its motion squeezed. Both curves use the paper's own formulas for a 27Al+ clock in a 20 MHz trap, one second of evolution.

Honesty note: the curves are the paper's formulas, not data — no experiment has run yet. Visibility V(r) = 1 − (εmωct)²/16 · sinh²(2r); the shift is Δν/ν = −(εm/4)·cosh(2r), with εm = 3.3 × 10−18 for the aluminium clock. The visibility formula is a small-loss approximation: past r ≈ 2.9 it would go negative, so the exhibit clamps it at zero — the formula breaking down, not a prediction. The qSODS — a still deeper quantum shift, a phase of order 10−10 radians — is not shown: the paper itself says it is too small to observe with current clocks.

The louder next step: boron

The effect grows as the ion gets lighter and the clock frequency gets higher. A 10B+ clock at 1,119 THz would, all else equal, drop the visibility to V ~ 0.76 — a 24% fade, impossible to miss. No such clock exists yet. It is the paper's own pointer to where the signal gets unmistakable.

The witness, not the proof

Visibility is a witness of entanglement, in the paper's careful word — the same role Zych et al. gave it in 2011. It is also possible, in principle, to verify the entanglement directly, by joint measurements on the motional and clock states, without relying on the visibility at all. The fringe fade is the cheapest way in; it is not the only way.

Chapter 3

The classical bill

Before the quantum version, there is the ordinary one — and it is worth feeling in the hands, because its smallness is the whole reason the quantum version matters. Time dilation at everyday speeds is not zero. It is just patient.

The bill is Δt ≈ t · v² / 2c². At a run, it takes geological time to add up to a second. At orbital speed it takes a year to cost you milliseconds. At a sizeable fraction of light speed it stops being subtle at all.

How much motion buys how much lag

Pick a speed and a duration. The readout is the time the moving clock loses against the clock that stayed home — special relativity only, no quantum anything.

Check: at 10 m/s for 57 million years this calculator returns 1.0 second — the Stevens example. At 7,800 m/s (low Earth orbit) for one year it returns about 10.7 milliseconds — the order of magnitude GPS satellites actually correct for, daily, along with the larger gravitational effect in the opposite direction.

Chapter 4

What would change this

A drawer that cannot say how it could be wrong is a story, not an exhibit. This one is a prediction with an address — the experiment has not run yet — so its kill conditions are the experiment's.

✓ Would confirm: a squeezed 27Al+ clock shows the visibility falling to ~0.93 at r = 2.26 and the frequency shift growing as cosh(2r) — both numbers moving together off the same knob. One number is a hint; two numbers on one curve is the effect.
× Would break the τ̂ picture: the squeezed clock keeps full visibility (V = 1) at r = 2.26, with the shift present or absent. If the fringes do not fade, the clock did not entangle with its motion, and the quantum proper-time dynamics the paper derives is not what happens in the trap.
× Would break the identification: the shift appears without the visibility drop, or the drop appears without the shift scaling as cosh(2r). A shift alone could be a systematic — a trap imperfection, a laser drift. The pair is the fingerprint; either half alone is a candidate, not a detection.
× Would break the "within reach" claim: if the squeezing or the one-second coherence cannot be held in practice — if decoherence eats the fringes before the entanglement can show. That would kill the experiment, not the theory; the paper's assumption is state-of-the-art coherence, and coherence is the thing that has to be defended in the lab.
What stays open: the qSODS — the deepest quantum signature in the paper, a phase of order 10−10 radians — is unobservable with current clocks and stays a prediction. And the whole drawer is a forecast: the team "now aims to demonstrate the effects in the laboratory." Until a fringe fades on the record, this is a clock that should disagree with itself.
Coda

The background, promoted

Time has always played two roles that do not fit together. In quantum mechanics it is a fixed parameter, the stage the experiment happens on. In relativity it is dynamical, something the experiment changes. The squeezed ion clock is where the two roles finally collide in a single instrument: the clock's motion is its time, and when the motion is quantum, the time is too.

This drawer grew out of a morning's half-thought — the instrument as participant. The clock's motion is part of its time; the channel is part of the speed; a mirror made of light makes matter behave like light at a horizon. The measurement arrangement is never a neutral frame. It co-authors the result. Here, the co-authorship has a number: 7%, at r = 2.26, waiting in a trap in Boulder for someone to squeeze hard enough to see it.
