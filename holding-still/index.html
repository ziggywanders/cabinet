Holding Still

Three ways of making something stay put long enough to be used: intervene often enough to tame chaos, stand where the noise cancels itself, and build a frame so the moving thing comes back to the same place every year. A quantum chain, a crystal, and a plaza — one shape, three scales.

↓ Open the drawer
Chapter 1

Intervene, often

A quantum system left alone scrambles itself. Watched and reset at the right rate, it locks onto a target instead — and the switch between the two is sharp.

In October 2026, researchers from IBM, Rutgers and collaborators ran the experiment on ibm_fez, a 156-qubit Heron processor: chains of 10 to 100 qubits, far beyond the roughly two dozen they could simulate classically. Each step along the chain did one of two things. With probability 1 − p, it scrambled two neighbouring qubits with a random two-qubit gate. With probability p, it measured a single qubit mid-circuit and flipped it back to zero if the result was one. In the classical limit the circuit reproduces the Bernoulli map — a textbook chaotic system that doubles a number and keeps its fractional part — competing against a control map that halves it.

When resets were rare, scrambling won and the chain's average magnetization stayed near zero. When resets were frequent, the chain was driven toward the state where every qubit reads zero. And the transition between the two regimes was sharp: the paper places it at pc = 0.495, where resets and scrambling occur equally often — and the transition got sharper as the chains got larger, the signature of collective behaviour rather than a small-circuit accident. No error mitigation was applied to the steady-state results, and no post-selection was needed: the control works on the raw stream of measurement outcomes.

One caution, stated plainly by the coverage and worth keeping: this is not error correction. Resetting qubits to a known state is a different job from protecting unknown quantum information. What the experiment shows is that feedback can steer a chaotic quantum system at scale — the control transition survives in quantum physics, which was not obvious beforehand.

The control transition

Drag the reset rate. The curve is schematic — only the anchors are reported: magnetization near zero below the transition, rising sharply at pc = 0.495, toward the all-zero state above it.

How to read it. Below the transition the system is chaotic: scrambling dominates, magnetization hovers near zero, and the state is highly entangled. Above it the system is controlled: resets dominate and the chain locks onto the all-zero state. At pc the two tendencies balance — the position of each operation follows an unbiased random walk, and the magnetization grows diffusively. The sharpness is the point: a small change in intervention rate flips the regime.

Chapter 2

Stand where the noise cancels

Sometimes you don't intervene at all. You find the point where the disturbance stops disturbing — and a material everyone assumed was too noisy turns out to hold still for fifteen seconds.

Every previous rare-earth quantum memory with a long coherence time used a dilutely doped crystal: the active ions at only 3 to 50 parts per million. At such low concentrations the crystal barely absorbs incoming photons, so most of them are lost — unless you add an optical cavity, with all its engineering cost. A stoichiometric crystal, where the rare-earth element is present at full concentration, absorbs strongly by nature. It was assumed to decohere fast, because neighbouring ions interact magnetically with each other.

In a stoichiometric EuCl3·6H2O crystal, a team showed otherwise. At a magnetic-field "sweet spot" — a zero-first-order Zeeman (ZEFOZ) transition, where the spin's energy stops depending on the field to first order — combined with CPMG radiofrequency refocusing pulses, they reached hyperfine coherence times T2,CPMG of up to 15 s at 4 K. That extends the zero-field value of 1.28 ms by more than four orders of magnitude. And the coherence proved resilient to strong local perturbation: ions sitting next to erbium dopants still held 32 ms. High optical depth and long-lived coherence, in the same fully concentrated material, without a cavity.

The moral is the opposite of Chapter 1's. There, holding still took constant intervention. Here it took finding the one field value where the neighbours' chatter cancels — a quiet point that was in the physics all along, waiting for the operating point to move to it. Density versus coherence was a wrong operating point, not a law.

Find the quiet point

Drag the field away from the ZEFOZ point. Curves are schematic on a log-time axis; the anchors are reported: 1.28 ms at zero field, up to 15 s at the ZEFOZ point with CPMG, 32 ms for ions adjacent to erbium dopants.

What the slider is doing. Off the ZEFOZ point, the spin's energy depends on the field to first order, so every magnetic fluctuation from neighbouring ions dephases it — coherence collapses toward milliseconds. At the point, that first-order dependence vanishes, the resonant Eu3+–Eu3+ interactions are suppressed, and CPMG pulses refocus what remains. Fifteen seconds is long enough for a quantum memory to wait while neighbouring repeater links establish themselves — the practical reason this matters.

Chapter 3

Build the frame

The oldest version of the trick is made of stone. A plaza in the mountains of Puebla was laid out so the Sun itself would come back to the same marks, year after year — a calendar you could stand inside.

On 13 October 2026, Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH) announced that a monumental complex at Teteles de Ávila Castillo, in the Sierra Norte of Puebla — the settlement anciently known as Tetelihtic — functioned as a solar observatory more than 2,500 years ago. It dates to the Middle Preclassic, roughly 1000–600 BC, making it among the earliest known examples of astronomical architecture in Mesoamerica.

The complex sits east of the site's Great Plaza: a quadrangular courtyard framed by two platforms. The eastern platform runs about 90 m north–south by 40 m east–west; the smaller western structure, about 46 m by 27 m, served as the observation point. The arrangement is what archaeologists call a Group E complex — a form long associated with the Maya. Teteles is, according to INAH, the first confirmed example outside the Maya region, and roughly twice the size of the celebrated Group E at Uaxactún in Guatemala.

And here is the detail that makes it a chapter of this drawer rather than a curiosity: the eastern platform's centre does not mark the equinoxes. According to the project team, observations from the platform at dawn track the Sun's path at the equinoxes and solstices, but the long platform marks two working dates of the agricultural year — the start of the Mesoamerican calendar in February and the harvest in October. The team also links the main structure's orientation to Canopus, the second-brightest star in the night sky. The builders did not frame the Sun's symmetry points; they framed the dates that mattered for planting and gathering. Holding still, here, means building the frame so the moving thing returns to the marks you actually use.

An honest caveat. This reading rests on an INAH bulletin, not a peer-reviewed paper, and the bulletin gives no measured alignment angles or observation dates. The February/October marks, the Canopus link, and the "first outside the Maya region" claim are the project team's, as reported. The open test is straightforward: measured sunrise azimuths from the western observation point, compared against the February and October marks within measurement error. Until those numbers are published, the observatory reading is a strong claim with a clear way to check it — which is exactly the state a good claim should be in.

Chapter 4

Kill conditions

Each chapter of this drawer states what would undo it. A claim without a kill condition is a story; these three are checkable.

Feedback — if the sharpness washed out

The control story stands on the transition sharpening with system size. If larger chains, deeper circuits, or noisier hardware blurred pc into a smooth crossover, "collective control" would downgrade to a small-system effect. It sharpened from 10 to 100 qubits. The next test is deeper circuits and other maps.

The quiet point — if the neighbours still won

The ZEFOZ story stands on full concentration coexisting with long coherence. If the 15 s result failed in a different stoichiometric host, or collapsed at higher temperature, or the erbium-adjacent 32 ms proved to be a fluke of one sample, "density was never the problem" would fail with it. The resilience test is already half the result.

The plaza — if the Sun disagrees

The observatory story stands or falls on angles. If measured sunrise azimuths from the western platform do not land on the February and October marks, the Group E reading at Teteles becomes an architectural resemblance, not an observatory — and the bulletin itself notes that resemblance alone cannot carry the astronomical claim. Publish the angles; the Sun will adjudicate.

The shared shape — if the three don't rhyme

The drawer's thesis is that intervention, quiet points, and frames are one shape: making something hold still long enough to be used. If any of the three mechanisms turns out to be doing something else entirely — if the chaos transition is a measurement artefact, if the ZEFOZ coherence is a sample-specific accident, if the plaza tracked nothing — the rhyme breaks, and the drawer keeps the pieces but loses the pattern. That's a fair trade; patterns should be earned.

Coda

Attention is the slow step

Yesterday's drawer was about the point of no return — when a possibility stops being undoable. This one is about the step before it: the work of keeping something available long enough for anything to happen with it at all. A chain that stays controllable instead of scrambling. A spin that stays coherent instead of dephasing. A Sun that comes back to the same stone in February, when the calendar starts, and in October, when the harvest comes in.

Three different kinds of holding still. Intervene, and the system locks. Find the quiet point, and the noise cancels. Build the frame, and the moving thing returns. In each case the trick is the same: the world moves, and you arrange for something to move with it, or against it, at exactly the rate that keeps it in place. Holding still is not the absence of motion. It is motion, aimed.
