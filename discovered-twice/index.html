Drawer Nº 18 · October 2026

Discovered Twice

Every new species is found twice: once when it is collected, and once — sometimes forty years later — when somebody finally looks closely enough to see that it was never named at all. The specimen sits in the jar. The discovery waits for the reader.

↓ open the drawer
Chapter 1

The year in the archives

The largest ocean-discovery program in the world just had its fastest year — and most of it happened indoors.

Between April 2025 and March 2026, the Nippon Foundation–Nekton Ocean Census recorded 1,121 marine species previously unknown to science — a 54% jump in the annual identification rate, across 13 expeditions and 9 specialist workshops. The headline number is real, but the geography of it is the surprise: 728 of those species came to light through Species Discovery Awards that paid taxonomists to work through recent expeditions and legacy backlogs — jars and drawers already sitting in collections, some for decades.

The bottleneck was never the diving. By the Ocean Census's own count, the average journey from collection to formal description takes about 13.5 years. The ocean is not short of new animals; it is short of people with the expertise and the funded hours to look at the ones already caught. That is why the program built NOVA, a platform that grants a formal “discovered” status — publicly logged within days or weeks of examination — distinct from the slower, formal “described” status a published taxonomic name confers. The 1,121 are candidates under that discovered status, not 1,121 finished descriptions. The distinction matters, and it is the honest version of the speed.

Somewhere right now, an animal nobody has named is sitting in a jar on a museum shelf, waiting to be found a second time.

Chapter 2

Two weeks in Buenos Aires

Fifty-seven new species and five new genera, from samples collected a year earlier — and livestreamed to millions.

In August 2025, the research vessel Falkor (too) sent the remotely operated vehicle SuBastian into the Mar del Plata Submarine Canyon off Argentina, and livestreamed the dives. More than four million people watched. Many came for a plump orange sea star that looked like Patrick Star from SpongeBob. Patrick, it turns out, is not a new species — scientists left him where he was, unaware of his fame.

The real haul surfaced a year later, on land. From July 13–24, 2026, twenty taxonomists and early-career researchers from Latin America and Europe gathered at the Universidad Maimónides in Buenos Aires for an Ocean Census Species Discovery Workshop and worked through the canyon samples with integrated taxonomy — morphology, high-resolution imaging, and DNA barcoding in parallel, logged straight onto NOVA. In two weeks they identified 57 species and 5 genera new to science: 20 copepods, 22 echinoderms, 14 cnidarians, and a single scaphopod, a tusk-shell mollusc.

The standout was “Loki,” a horned sea cucumber (Peniagone sp.) filmed at 2,577 metres on the canyon floor — one of up to ten new holothurian species from the workshop. The canyon had been watched by millions; it had not been read until the workshop sat down with the jars.

Chapter 3

The teaching specimen

It was on display. It had a name. Both were wrong.

In the late 1980s, the Tübingen paleontologist Dolf Seilacher acquired a giant isopod at a marine research station in Puerto Rico — pulled from a trap 500 metres down — and brought it home for teaching. For decades it sat in the University of Tübingen's Paleontological Collection filed as Bathynomus giganteus, the well-known Gulf of Mexico species, a 32.2-centimetre roly-poly scaled up to the length of a forearm. It was even a central object in an exhibition, “GIGANTIC in the sea,” at Rosenstein Castle in Stuttgart from October 2019 to June 2020 — displayed, admired, and still mislabeled.

What had never been done was a close look at its mouthparts, which descriptions in this group often skip. In 2026, Tübingen student Benedikt Kästle and curator Ingmar Werneburg photographed it, ran it through an X-ray micro-CT scanner, and compared its anatomy against giant isopods worldwide. The verdict, published October 8, 2026, in Scientific Reports: a species nobody had named — Bathynomus tuebingoborikensis, from Boríken, the Taíno name for Puerto Rico, and Tübingen, the city where it had been sitting. At 32.2 cm it falls in the authors' newly coined “colossogiant” class, animals at least 255 mm long.

The same pattern repeats at smaller scales. A deep-sea coral first spotted off Molokaʻi in 2006 waited until 2025 for its name: Iridogorgia chewbacca. Nineteen years between sighting and naming — not because the coral was hidden, but because nobody with the right expertise had yet done the describing. Discovery, here, was not an expedition. It was an appointment.

Chapter 4

The trench predators

Below the depth where fishes give out, something else is on top.

Princaxelia are predatory amphipods of the hadal trenches — deeper than 6,000 metres — usually caught in small numbers, which is why their taxonomy has stayed uncertain. In August 2026, ZooKeys 1289 added two species, and the pair rewrites the genus's story in two directions at once.

Princaxelia malohi comes from the Tonga Trench, five specimens taken by baited landers at 8,200–8,350 metres during the 2024 Inkfish expedition aboard RV Dagon — the first Princaxelia ever recorded from that trench. Princaxelia kahat comes from the Mariana Trench, at 8,000–8,964 metres — but its specimens were collected in 2014, from landers deployed off RV Falkor, and waited twelve years for description. The two new species live in different trenches; the coexistence story is inside the Mariana, where kahat, hunting boulder fields below 8,000 metres, now shares a trench system with the earlier P. marianaensis, collected from cold seeps at 5,683 metres — niche-partitioned by depth and habitat, the first evidence of more than one Princaxelia in a single hadal feature.

Below about 8,300 metres, fishes reach their lower limit and disappear. Down there, Princaxelia — locally abundant where prey gathers around food falls — may be among the top predators left. The deepest food webs on Earth are still being staffed, one jar at a time.

Chapter 5

Kill conditions

What would change this drawer — including the drawer itself.

“Discovered” is not “described”

If formal descriptions revise the Ocean Census tally — species sunk as synonyms, candidates re-identified — the 1,121 shrinks. The honest count is candidates under NOVA's discovered status, and this drawer stands or falls with that distinction kept visible.

The backlog could drain

If funded taxonomy workshops keep running at the Buenos Aires pace, the 13.5-year average lag collapses — and “discovered twice” stops being the normal story. That would be the drawer succeeding itself out of existence.

The trench pair could split — or merge

If new Princaxelia captures show kahat and marianaensis overlapping in depth and habitat, the niche-partition reading weakens; if barcodes merge either new species into an older one, the count of seven falls back. The genus key in ZooKeys 1289 is built to be tested by the next lander.

For the drawer itself

If a reader shows that a “second discovery” here was actually continuous — a specimen studied all along, a name merely delayed in print — that chapter's framing is wrong, and the drawer should say so. The record stabilizes before the reader; the reader's job is to admit when they arrived late.
