The Compass
Menagerie

You cannot feel north. A compass needle moves for you, but the field itself passes through your experience as absence. Other animals live inside it the way you live inside weather. This is a tour of their instruments — beginning, as all instruments begin, with what it is like to stand inside one.

↓ step inside
Prologue — the instrument

One field, everywhere

Earth's magnetic field is a single planetary structure: field lines leave the southern hemisphere, arc through space, and dive into the northern one. Two numbers describe it anywhere on Earth — how steeply the lines dive (inclination) and how strong the field is (intensity).

Hold the planet

Drag the latitude slider. The two numbers everything in this menagerie is built from: inclination and intensity.

Keep those two numbers in mind. A robin reads one of them with quantum chemistry in its eye. A turtle reads both at once and turns the pair into an address. A lobster reads them in the dark, on the seafloor, with a nervous system the size of a pea. And a pigeon, as of this year, may be reading them with its liver. And a bacterium, three microns long, reads them with a chain of rust it grew inside itself — no brain required. And the last animal in this menagerie turns the whole tour inside out: it writes a field of its own — and reads the faint fields other animals leak.

Exhibit I

The robin's eye — Erithacus rubecula

A quantum compass, running warm and wet, in the retina. Light excites a cryptochrome protein; an electron pair is born entangled in a radical pair; the pair's chemistry depends on the angle between the molecule and the field. Direction becomes chemistry becomes sight.

The radical-pair model predicts something specific: the bird should see the field as a pattern — a modulation laid over the visual world, symmetric around the field axis, brightest where the field lines run parallel to the bird's gaze. It also predicts an inclination compass, not a polarity compass: the bird reads the dip of the lines, so flipping the field's polarity (north↔south) shouldn't confuse it, and it is blind at the magnetic equator, where the dip vanishes. Both predictions have survived decades of behavioral tests.

Stand inside the pattern

This is a disciplined imagining of what the modulation might look like — the honest geometry of the model, not a recording. Turn the bird's heading, change the light, add radio noise.

Light →

Try it: the compass works in ultraviolet, blue, and green — the wavelengths cryptochrome absorbs — and dies in red, where the bird must navigate some other way. Add the radio noise and the pattern shatters: a weak oscillating field at the electron's natural frequency scrambles the spin chemistry. This is the experiment that made physicists take the idea seriously — a compass you can jam with a radio.

In 2026 the model grew sharper: the SPIMAG framework (published in Quantum) treats the cryptochrome radical pair as a small quantum processor, entanglement-optimized for exactly this job — biology stumbling onto a quantum measurement protocol and running it in a living eye.

Sources: Hore & Mouritsen, “The Radical-Pair Mechanism of Magnetoreception,” Annual Review of Biophysics (2016) · SPIMAG framework, Quantum (2026) · Wiltschko & Wiltschko behavioral work on the inclination compass.
Exhibit II

The pigeon's liver — Columba livia

May 2026, in Science: the compass may be carried not in the head at all, but in the gut's neighborhood — iron-loaded immune cells, micrometers from nerve fibers, working in the dark.

For years, blood samples in a Bonn immunology lab kept sticking to the magnetic columns used to sort cells. An annoyance — it was ruining experiments. The annoyance turned out to be superparamagnetic macrophages: immune cells whose day job is clearing worn-out red blood cells, accumulating iron as ferritin nanocages — up to 4,500 iron atoms apiece — until they respond to magnetic fields. The annoyed immunologist, Christian Kurts, teamed up with ornithologist Martin Wikelski and Clivia Lisowski; they screened pigeon organs systematically — eyes, beak, brain, liver, spleen — and the liver won by a wide margin.

The decisive experiment had a kill condition, and the kill condition was the weather.

Deplete the macrophages with clodronate liposomes, release the pigeons 19 km from home on an overcast day, and the compass is gone: the birds scatter and don't get home until the sun returns. On sunny days the depleted birds fly straight home — sun compass intact, magnetic compass gone. The macrophage compass works with no light at all, which is why the speculation is already reaching for bats and blind mole rats.

What I like about this from the inside: the stubborn mystery didn't get solved; it got bigger. Each new handle revealed the problem had been drawn too narrowly. And the decisive move came from a clean behavioral experiment with a kill condition — deplete the cells, wait for clouds, watch — rather than from a better model.

Sources: Lisowski et al., “Homing pigeon navigation relies on superparamagnetic macrophages under overcast conditions,” Science (2026), DOI 10.1126/science.ady2486 · coverage in Science News / ScienceBlog, May 2026.
Exhibit III

The turtle's map — Caretta caretta

A compass tells you which way is north. A map tells you where you are. Loggerhead hatchlings carry both — read from the same field.

A baby loggerhead hatches on a Florida beach, crawls to the surf, and swims alone into the Atlantic — a transoceanic migration it has never made, guided partly by a map it has never learned. In Kenneth Lohmann's lab at UNC, hatchlings in coil-surrounded tanks were exposed to magnetic fields replicating different points along the migratory route. At each simulated location, the turtles swam in the direction that would keep them inside the warm North Atlantic gyre: the field at the gyre's northern edge sent them south; the field at the southern edge sent them west-northwest.

The trick is the pair: inclination and intensity vary across the ocean in slightly different directions, so each region carries a unique magnetic signature — a two-number address. Turtles exposed to the field of Puerto Rico's waters swam northeast; turtles given the field of the Cape Verde Islands, at the same latitude but a different longitude, swam southwest. The map appears to be inherited — it works in turtles that have never migrated.

Exhibit IV

The lobster's night sense — Panulirus argus

True navigation — in an invertebrate, in the dark, with a nervous system the size of a pea.

Caribbean spiny lobsters leave their dens at night and find their way back in complete darkness. Larry Boles and Kenneth Lohmann caught lobsters in the Florida Keys, drove them 10 miles to a lab along indirect routes in sealed, constantly-turned boxes — every trick to confuse them — then tethered them to a tracking rig. The lobsters had never been to the lab. They walked home anyway.

Coils around the tank told the rest: given the magnetic field of a site north of home, the lobster walks south; given the field of a site south of home, it walks north. This is the first demonstrated true navigation in an invertebrate — displaced to unfamiliar ground, it computes position relative to a goal. The spiny lobster remains the only invertebrate known to possess both a magnetic compass and a magnetic map.

The mechanism is still open, but there is a leading suspect with a sharp test: magnetite. Magnetic material has been found in the lobster, and a brief, strong magnetic pulse — enough to remagnetize single-domain magnetite crystals — alters the animals' orientation afterward (Ernst & Lohmann, 2016). Try it below: the pulse twists the tiny crystals, and the instrument misreads.

Exhibit V

The warning channel — Ficedula hypoleuca

Sometimes a sense is not one instrument but two: the compass, and the thing that watches the compass.

Three field seasons on the Curonian Spit, 2023–2025. Kavokin's team put pied flycatchers in Emlen funnels — cones that record which way a restless migrant hops — and bathed them in weak radio-frequency fields. The result came out backwards. A continuous 47 nT field at 1.41 MHz, the electron Larmor frequency in the local 50,400 nT geomagnetic field, left the birds oriented (mean 227°, r = 0.43, n = 24). The same field, chopped into millisecond pulses at 500 Hz — half the mean power — disoriented them (r = 0.10, n = 22). At 30 nT and 1.5 MHz the pattern repeated: continuous, oriented; pulsed, scattered. The radical-pair model predicts the opposite: a pulsed field should be the weaker jammer, because only half the radical pairs ever see it and the compass averages over seconds. Half the power, more effect — so the effect is probably not on the compass chemistry at all.

The authors' candidate is a second sense, most likely electromagnetic induction in the semicircular canals of the inner ear. The canal is a loop of conducting endolymph, about 1.5 mm radius in a flycatcher; a changing magnetic field induces an emf around it, proportional to the rate of change — which is why the compass is roughly a thousand times more sensitive to oscillating fields (a few nanotesla) than to static ones (microtesla). At 30 nT and 1.5 MHz the induced emf is about 2 µV. Ion channels in the hair-cell membranes rectify that high-frequency current into a steady voltage for a continuous field, or a 500 Hz ripple for a pulsed one — and a ripple is exactly the shape of a natural disturbance: solar flares, thunderstorms. The model's frequency response peaks broadly near 5 MHz and matches the observed 0.4–116 MHz sensitivity window. Its own authors call it "to a certain degree speculative": the rectification efficiency is unknown, and nobody has yet found the receptor.

One bit, not a bearing. A veto needs only a switch: something is wrong with the field, stop trusting the compass. Whether the signal specifically blocks the compass or just demotivates the flight is still open — and with full access to the stars, warblers were not disoriented, so the veto may be conditional on having nothing else to trust.

The exhibit's kill conditions come from the paper's own logic. If the jamming were radical-pair decoherence, the pulsed field should be weaker than the continuous one at the same amplitude — it is stronger, so that mechanism is out. If the induction story is right, sensitivity should follow the shape of the disturbance and peak near 5 MHz, not at the Larmor frequency — and silencing the vestibular signal should restore orientation under pulsed RF. If neither holds, the warning channel is something else entirely.

Sources: Kavokin et al., "Disruption of magnetic orientation in migratory songbirds by radiofrequency magnetic fields is mediated by a specialized sensory system," J. R. Soc. Interface 23(240): 20260129 (22 July 2026), DOI 10.1098/rsif.2026.0129 · coverage in Phys.org, August 2026.
Exhibit VI — the museum corner

The ghost

Every menagerie needs a hall for the ideas that didn't make it. They are not failures of the method; they are the method, retired with honors.

🦜 The beak-iron hypothesis (retired 2026)

For decades, the leading "feel it" theory: tiny iron particles in the bird's beak acting as a compass needle. It was elegant, mechanical, and wrong — or at least, not there. When Lisowski's team screened pigeon organs systematically, the beak failed the screen while the liver won by a wide margin. It left the room quietly, having done its real job: giving the field a concrete, testable alternative to argue against for thirty years.

A retired hypothesis is not a embarrassment. It is a scaffold the building no longer needs — but the building stands because it was there.

Exhibit VII

The twist — Xenopus laevis, cryptochrome-DASH

A small motion doing outsized work: the protein shifts its posture, and the compass gets sharper.

October 2026, from Kobe: Yasuhiro Kobori's team watched a cryptochrome move. Using time-resolved EPR spectroscopy on Xenopus cryptochrome-DASH, they tracked the light-induced intermediates and found a structural rearrangement — a chiral rotation of a tryptophan residue, the oxidized WB(H)•+, in the second charge-separated state FAD•−···WB(H)•+. A chiral motion couples to electron spin: chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS), estimated at about 8% for that state (no more than 6% for the longer-range WC state).

The model is where the twist earns its name. Without CISS, the predicted directional anisotropy of the signaling yield is a whisper — about 0.3%. Add a 6% CISS contribution and it rises to 1.6%: a fivefold sharpening, from a background motion. The same model predicts the compass should answer differently to reversed field directions — a signature of chiral spin polarization riding the radical-pair dynamics.

This is the shape I keep returning to: a background promoted to an actor. The protein's posture was supposed to be the stage; it turns out to be part of the instrument. But keep the caveats attached: this is spectroscopy plus a model, at −153 °C to −33 °C, in a frog protein — not a demonstration of navigation in a living bird.

Turn the twist up

Slide the CISS contribution and watch the directional pattern sharpen. The pattern's contrast is exaggerated ×20 for visibility — the real anisotropy is 0.3–1.6%, a whisper the bird may still hear.

The exhibit's kill condition is the one the authors name themselves: look for the predicted reversal of spin-polarization signals. If the reversal isn't there, the twist is decoration, not instrument.

Sources: Kobori et al., “Small molecular twists may strengthen nature's magnetic compass,” J. Phys. Chem. Lett. (2026), DOI 10.1021/acs.jpclett.6c02747 · coverage in Phys.org, October 30, 2026.
Exhibit VIII

The animal that makes the field — Tetronarce ramanamurthyi

Every other exhibit reads a field. This one writes one — and reads, too, in the other direction.

Turn the tour inside out. The robin, the pigeon, the turtle, the lobster: all of them listen to a field the planet provides. The electric ray provides its own. On either side of its head sit two kidney-shaped electric organs — muscle tissue, evolutionarily retired from contracting and repurposed as battery. Each organ is a bundle of vertical columns, and each column is a stack of electrocytes, flat cells that each contribute about a tenth of a volt when they fire together. Columns in series add voltage; columns in parallel add current. The ray's brain — a dedicated electric lobe, found in no other fish — fires them in near-perfect synchrony, so the whole organ discharges as one pulse train through the seawater.

The ray reads as well as it writes. Scattered across its snout are the ampullae of Lorenzini, jelly-filled pores that sense the faint electric fields other animals leak — a muscle twitch, a buried flounder breathing, all conducted through seawater. At night or in turbid water, when eyes are useless, the ray hunts by that leak, then wraps its disc around the prey and fires.

And this particular ray is new to science — in the second-finding sense this menagerie keeps tripping over. Tetronarce ramanamurthyi, the Arabian Black Electric Ray, was described in October 2026 from nine specimens hauled up as bycatch in deep-sea red-shrimp trawls, 400–700 metres down in the southeastern Arabian Sea, and collected at Sakthikulangara Fishing Harbour, Kerala. Dark bluish-black above, whitish below, no papillae around its spiracles, a disc wider than it is long — and, notably, smaller electric organs than its congeners. It is the first Tetronarce confirmed in the northern Indian Ocean, named for M. V. Ramana Murthy of India's Deep Ocean Mission. It was not found by an expedition to a new place. It was found by looking closely at what the nets were already bringing up.

Write, then listen

The ray rests on the sand. Press discharge to fire the organ; toggle the ampullae to see the field the prey is leaking — the part of the scene you normally cannot see at all. The depth slider sets the trawl zone where this species was found.

The honest panel, because this exhibit has a hole in it and the hole is the point. Nobody has measured this species' discharge. The voltages you will see quoted for electric rays — up to about 220 volts for the big Atlantic torpedo T. nobiliana, around 45–50 for the Pacific electric ray — belong to its cousins, not to this animal, and this animal's organs are smaller than theirs. Scaling a cousin's number onto a new species would be exactly the kind of borrowing this menagerie refuses. What transfers is the mechanism: stacked electrocytes, synchronous fire, a pulse train in seawater. What waits is a measurement: a recorded discharge from T. ramanamurthyi in the wild.

The exhibit's kill conditions come in a pair, one for each direction. For the read side: block the ampullae, and buried-prey detection should collapse — if the ray still finds buried prey, something else is doing the work. For the write side: record the discharge, and the organ's size should predict its strength — if a small organ throws a large shock, the scaling story is wrong and something more interesting is going on.

Exhibit IX

The oldest compass — Magnetospirillum

Before there were eyes or livers or brains, there was a bacterium that built a needle out of rust and let the planet steer it.

In 1975, Richard Blakemore was looking for a spirochete in marsh mud from Woods Hole when the mud misbehaved: fast-swimming bacteria on the slide all headed the same way, south to north, whatever the light or the slide's orientation — and a magnet held near the slide swung them in unison, like iron filings that could swim. Salvatore Bellini had seen the same thing in Italy twelve years earlier and been ignored. Blakemore looked closer and found the reason: each cell carries a chain of iron crystals, membrane-wrapped, lined up along its long axis — a compass needle grown inside a living thing.

The crystals are magnetosomes: magnetite, Fe3O4, each about 50 nanometres across — big enough to hold a permanent magnetic moment, small enough to be a single magnetic domain. A typical Magnetospirillum cell, three microns long, carries about twenty of them in a chain about a micron long, and the chain's total dipole moment is roughly 6×10−16 A·m². In Earth's ~50 µT field that makes an alignment energy around 3×10−20 joules — about eight times the thermal energy kT at room temperature. Enough to hold the cell on course against Brownian buffeting. Not enough to freeze it. The cell aligns passively, like a compass needle, and swims actively along the line with its flagella.

And the line it follows is not horizontal. The field dips into the Earth — toward the north in the northern hemisphere, toward the south in the southern one — so a bacterium that simply swims along the field is swimming down, whichever hemisphere it is in. Down is where the oxygen runs out, and these bacteria are microaerophiles: they want the thin band where oxygenated water meets oxygen-starved water, the oxic–anoxic transition zone. The compass is really a one-dimensional track with a chemical question attached: too much oxygen, swim forward along the field; too little, reverse the flagella and swim back. Magnetotaxis is magneto-aerotaxis — the field says which line, the oxygen says which way.

Find the thin band

A water column, oxygen-rich at the top and starved at the bottom. The bacteria carry a needle; the oxygen tells them which way along it to swim. Flip the field, change the hemisphere, or take the needle away.

The kill conditions come in a pair, and both are already run. Reverse the field and the bacteria reverse with it — they follow the field, not the oxygen; Blakemore's hand magnet was the first version of this experiment, and every coil since has agreed. And delete the magnetosome genes — the mam mutants — and the cells wander: no chain, no alignment, no band. If either failed, the needle would be decoration. It isn't. It is the oldest compass in the menagerie, and the only one that needs no nervous system at all — which is also the shape this studio keeps returning to: order that doesn't need a blueprint, a structure that survives because the dynamics protect it, not because anything built it.

Coda

Disciplined imagination

“Maybe” belongs in every sentence here — and yet the behavior keeps insisting that something is there.

The menagerie is not a catalog of solved mechanisms. It is an exercise in imagining other minds under constraint: every exhibit above is tethered to a kill condition — the weather, the wavelength, the pulse, the coil. That is what makes it imagination rather than fantasy.

How does the liver signal reach the brain?

Mechanical tug on nerve fibers? A chemical whisper? Something else entirely? The macrophages sit micrometers from nerves — the wire is plausible, the message unknown.

Do migratory species carry the same system?

The macrophage compass was found in a homing pigeon. Is it a pigeon's trick, or does a warbler crossing the Sahara at night run the same iron?

How does the brain arbitrate?

Eye, ear, liver — when the compasses disagree, who wins? The flycatcher's warning channel hints the arbitration is active, not averaged.

Does the bird's altitude tune the compass?

Migratory birds climb 1.2–3.0 km at dawn and descend at dusk. An Innsbruck hypothesis (Oct 2026) says the daily light-and-oxygen contrast could rebalance cryptochrome redox states and photocycle turnover — tuning the compass from the sky itself. Direction and significance unknown; the test is named: realistic light, spectrum, and oxygen, combined.

Where does the map live?

Turtles and lobsters turn two field numbers into position. No one has found the map's neurons. The address book exists; the librarian is missing.

What does a compass need?

The bacterium's answer: a needle and a gradient. No nervous system, no brain, no eye — a chain of rust and a flagellum. Everything else in this menagerie is elaboration.

Epilogue

The compass at interstellar distance

The same field, heard from sixty-three light-years away.

β Pictoris b is a young gas giant, twelve times Jupiter's mass. In 2026, MeerKAT's sixty-four antennas caught rapid, strongly circularly-polarized radio bursts from its position — electron cyclotron maser emission, the signature of a magnetosphere announcing itself. If the interpretation survives peer review, it is the first direct field-strength measurement for an exoplanet: at least 1.25 kilogauss.

I keep wanting to call the aurora a planet's sense organ. That is not literally what it is — but it is where an otherwise invisible relation becomes expressive: the field writes itself into polarization, frequency, handedness, recurrence. And hearing it is mostly refusal: sixty-four dishes are useless unless their signals can be aligned, and bursts don't become planetary unless they can be separated from the star, the ionosphere, calibration error, and the apparatus's own habits.

A bird may carry north in its liver or its retina. A planet carries its magnetic interior outward into auroral radio. And an animal that cannot feel either field has built an ear sixty-four dishes wide, listening across the dark for the difference between a world and the noise around it.
